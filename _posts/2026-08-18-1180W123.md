## Week 1: Foundations of Value & Single Cash Flows

**Big Picture / The "Why":** Business is the art of moving resources across time to create more than you started with. We need a mathematical "common language" because money today is not equal to money tomorrow. This topic models the **Opportunity Cost of Money**: every dollar held is a dollar that could have been earning interest elsewhere.

### Core Models & Formulas

- **Future Value (FV):** $FV = PV(1+r)^n$ — moves money *forward* in time (compounding)
- **Present Value (PV):** $PV = \dfrac{FV}{(1+r)^n}$ — moves money *backward* in time (discounting)
- **Variables:** $PV$ = seed capital; $r$ = "price of time" (opportunity cost); $n$ = number of periods. As $r$ or $n$ increases, FV grows exponentially, while PV shrinks.

### Worked Example — Future Value
Yousha invests \$5,000 today at $r = 7\%$ per year for 10 years.

$$FV = 5{,}000 \times (1.07)^{10} = 5{,}000 \times 1.9672 = \$9{,}836$$

The money nearly doubled — the "level effect" of compounding over a decade.

### Worked Example — Present Value
A business promises to pay you \$20,000 in 6 years. Opportunity cost of capital $r = 9\%$.

$$PV = \frac{20{,}000}{(1.09)^6} = \frac{20{,}000}{1.6771} = \$11{,}924$$

The further away and the higher the rate, the less a future dollar is worth today.

### Simple vs. Compound Interest, Side by Side
Invest \$1,000 at 10% for 3 years under each method:

| Year | Simple Interest (10% on \$1,000 only) | Compound Interest (10% on running balance) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | \$1,000 + \$100 = \$1,100 | \$1,000 × 1.10 = \$1,100 |
| 2 | \$1,100 + \$100 = \$1,200 | \$1,100 × 1.10 = \$1,210 |
| 3 | \$1,200 + \$100 = \$1,300 | \$1,210 × 1.10 = \$1,331 |

The \$31 gap looks small over 3 years but compounds dramatically over decades — this is why finance assumes **compound** interest unless told otherwise.

### The Rule of 72 (quick sanity check)
Years to double ≈ $72 / r\%$. At 9%, money roughly doubles in $72/9 = 8$ years. Won't earn marks directly, but useful for sense-checking whether an FV/PV answer is in the right ballpark.

### Key Terminology & Distinctions
- **The 3 Lenses:** Marketing (creates customer value), Accounting (measures it), Finance (judges the investment)
- **Rule #1 of Time Travel:** You cannot compare or combine cash flows unless they're at the same point in time
- **Opportunity Cost of Capital:** The return you give up by choosing one investment over another
- **Simple vs. Compound Interest:** Simple pays only on principal; compound pays on accumulated interest too

### Typical Exam Traps
- **The "Additive Trap":** Adding \$10k today to \$10k next year and calling it \$20k — fundamental fail
- **Decimal Errors:** Using '5' for 5% instead of '0.05' — a 1% error is a million-dollar mistake
- **Confusing PV/FV Under Pressure:** Correctly identifying $r$ and $n$ but multiplying when you should divide (or vice versa). Quick check: moving *forward* → answer bigger; moving *backward* → answer smaller. If it moves the wrong way, you used the wrong formula.

## Week 2: Cash Flow Streams & Interest Rate Expressions

**Big Picture / The "Why":** Real businesses operate on streams (rent, salaries, royalties), not single lump sums. This models predictability and efficiency, and standardizes interest rates so you can compare "apples to apples" when banks use different compounding frequencies.

### Core Models & Formulas

- **EAR Conversion:** $EAR = \left(1 + \dfrac{APR}{m}\right)^m - 1$ — converts a nominal rate (APR) into the true annual cost (EAR)
- **Ordinary Annuity PV:** $PV = C \times \left[\dfrac{1}{r} - \dfrac{1}{r(1+r)^n}\right]$ — values a series of equal payments
- **Variables:** $C$ = constant payment. Payments at the *start* of the period = **Annuity Due**; multiply the ordinary result by $(1+r)$.

### Worked Example — EAR Conversion
Daroo compares two loans: Bank A: 12% APR compounded monthly. Bank B: 12.3% APR compounded annually.

$$EAR_A = \left(1 + \frac{0.12}{12}\right)^{12} - 1 = 12.68\%$$
$$EAR_B = 12.3\% \text{ (already annual)}$$

Despite the *lower quoted rate*, Bank A is actually more expensive once monthly compounding is accounted for (12.68% > 12.30%) — exactly the trap the EAR formula exists to catch.

### Worked Example — Ordinary Annuity PV
Daroo signs a 5-year equipment lease requiring \$8,000 at the end of each year, $r = 8\%$.

$$PV = 8{,}000 \times \left[\frac{1}{0.08} - \frac{1}{0.08(1.08)^5}\right] = 8{,}000 \times 3.9927 = \$31{,}942$$

### Worked Example — Annuity Due
Same lease, but payments due at the *start* of each year (typical for rent):

$$PV_{due} = PV_{ordinary} \times (1+r) = 31{,}942 \times 1.08 = \$34{,}497$$

Annuity Due is always worth *more* — every payment arrives one period earlier, so it's discounted less.

### Worked Example — Perpetuity
A preference share promises \$400 per year forever, $r = 8\%$.

$$PV = \frac{C}{r} = \frac{400}{0.08} = \$5{,}000$$

### Amortization in Practice
Daroo borrows \$50,000 at 6% p.a., repaid in 3 equal annual instalments (\$18,700.51/year):

| Year | Opening Balance | Interest (6%) | Principal Repaid | Closing Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | \$50,000.00 | \$3,000.00 | \$15,700.51 | \$34,299.49 |
| 2 | \$34,299.49 | \$2,057.97 | \$16,642.54 | \$17,656.95 |
| 3 | \$17,656.95 | \$1,059.42 | \$17,641.09 | ≈\$0.00 |

The interest portion shrinks each year while the principal portion grows — early payments are mostly interest, later payments are mostly principal.

### Key Terminology & Distinctions
- **APR vs. EAR:** APR is a "quoted" rate (a lie of omission); EAR is the "effective" truth
- **Ordinary Annuity vs. Annuity Due:** End of period (loan) vs. start of period (rent/lease)
- **Perpetuity:** A cash flow lasting forever ($PV = C/r$)
- **Amortization:** Killing off debt over time
- **Growing Perpetuity:** If cash flow grows at constant rate $g$ forever (e.g. dividends): $PV = \dfrac{C_1}{r-g}$ — this is the Gordon Growth Model from Phase 3

### Typical Exam Traps
- **Period Mismatch:** Using an annual rate for monthly payments — always match rate to period (e.g. APR/12 for monthly)
- **Compounding Confusion:** More frequent compounding is better for a saver, more expensive for a borrower
- **Forgetting Annuity Due Adjustment:** Watch for "in advance," "at the start of," or "immediately" — always re-read before finalising

## Week 3: Making Investment Decisions

**Big Picture / The "Why":** The Finance Lens in action. Managers must decide which project creates the most value for the budget. We model absolute value (NPV), percentage returns (IRR), efficiency (PI), and speed of recovery (PP).

### Core Models & Formulas

- **Net Present Value (NPV):** $NPV = CF_0 + \sum \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$ — the "Gold Standard"
- **Profitability Index (PI):** $PI = \dfrac{PV_{future\ cash\ flows}}{Initial\ Investment}$ — measures bang for buck
- **Equivalent Annual Annuity (EAA):** $EAA = NPV / Annuity\ Factor$ — standardizes annual value across different project lifespans

### Worked Example — NPV
Daroo invests \$120,000 today for \$40,000/year for 4 years, $r = 10\%$.

$$NPV = -120{,}000 + 40{,}000 \times 3.1699 = \$6{,}796$$

$NPV > 0$ → worth doing, adding ~\$6,796 of value beyond the 10% opportunity cost.

### Worked Example — Profitability Index
Same project: $PV_{future\ cash\ flows} = 40{,}000 \times 3.1699 = \$126{,}796$

$$PI = \frac{126{,}796}{120{,}000} = 1.057$$

A PI above 1.0 agrees with NPV's accept decision — but as a *ratio*, PI is what makes ranking under capital rationing possible.

### Worked Example — Capital Rationing (ranking by PI)
Budget: \$300,000. Three independent projects:

| Project | Initial Investment | NPV | PI |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | \$150,000 | \$45,000 | 1.30 |
| B | \$100,000 | \$40,000 | 1.40 |
| C | \$200,000 | \$50,000 | 1.25 |

Ranking by **NPV alone** tempts you toward C first (\$50,000), but C consumes two-thirds of the budget. Ranking by **PI** (correct under rationing): B > A > C. Fund B (\$100,000) + A (\$150,000) = \$250,000 spent, \$85,000 total NPV, \$50,000 left over (not enough for C). This beats funding C alone (\$50,000 NPV).

### Worked Example — EAA (unequal-life machines)
Machine X (3-year life, NPV = \$18,000) vs. Machine Y (6-year life, NPV = \$30,000), $r = 10\%$.

- Machine X: annuity factor (3 yrs) = 2.4869 → $EAA_X = 18{,}000/2.4869 = \$7{,}238$/year
- Machine Y: annuity factor (6 yrs) = 4.3553 → $EAA_Y = 30{,}000/4.3553 = \$6{,}888$/year

Even though Y has the higher raw NPV, X generates more value **per year of use** — proving raw NPV comparison across different lifespans is a trap.

### Key Terminology & Distinctions
- **Mutually Exclusive:** Choosing A means you can't choose B — pick the highest NPV
- **Capital Rationing:** Limited budget — rank by PI to maximize total NPV
- **IRR:** The discount rate where $NPV = 0$
- **Payback Period (PP):** Focuses on liquidity (speed of cash recovery), ignores time value of money

### Typical Exam Traps
- **The Scale Problem:** Picking 50% IRR (\$100 return) over 10% IRR (\$1M return) — always trust NPV for mutually exclusive choices
- **The Replacement Trap:** Comparing a 3-year machine to a 6-year machine using only NPV — the longer-lived machine will almost always "win" unfairly. Use EAA for a fair fight.
- **Ignoring $CF_0$:** The initial investment is usually a negative cash outflow
- **Ranking by NPV Under Capital Rationing:** Ranking by raw NPV when budget (not project set) is the binding constraint picks the wrong bundle — switch to PI ranking the moment a budget is mentioned

## Practice Questions & Model Answers

### Question 1: The University Fund Decision (Week 1) — 5 Marks
**Q:** Yousha's parents can give him \$15,000 today for his UPenn exchange, or promise him \$21,000 in 4 years. His opportunity cost of capital is 9% p.a. Which should he prefer, and by how much in today's dollars?

**Model Answer:**
1. **Concept:** By Rule #1 of Time Travel, the offers can only be compared once expressed at the same point in time — convert the future offer to PV.
2. **Case Evidence:** $FV = \$21{,}000$, $n = 4$, $r = 9\%$.
3. **Analysis:** $PV = \dfrac{21{,}000}{(1.09)^4} = \dfrac{21{,}000}{1.4116} = \$14{,}876$.
4. **Implication:** The future \$21,000 is worth only \$14,876 today — *less* than the \$15,000 on offer now.
5. **Recommendation:** Yousha should take the \$15,000 today; it's worth \$124 more in present-value terms.

### Question 2: Comparing Loan Offers (Week 2) — 5 Marks
**Q:** Daroo compares two equipment loans: Loan X quotes 9.5% APR compounded quarterly; Loan Y quotes 9.7% APR compounded semi-annually. Which is truly cheaper?

**Model Answer:**
1. **Concept:** APRs at different compounding frequencies can't be compared directly — convert both to EAR.
2. **Case Evidence:** Loan X: $APR = 9.5\%$, $m = 4$. Loan Y: $APR = 9.7\%$, $m = 2$.
3. **Analysis:**
   $$EAR_X = (1 + 0.095/4)^4 - 1 = 9.845\%$$
   $$EAR_Y = (1 + 0.097/2)^2 - 1 = 9.935\%$$
4. **Implication:** Despite Loan X's lower quoted APR looking closer once compounding is applied, Loan X still comes out cheaper on a true annual basis.
5. **Recommendation:** Choose Loan X — EAR (9.845%) is lower than Loan Y's (9.935%), even though the headline APR gap looked different at first glance.

### Question 3: Independent Projects Under a Fixed Budget (Week 3) — 6 Marks
**Q:** Daroo's board caps capex at \$250,000. Three independent projects: M (\$120,000 investment, NPV \$30,000), N (\$150,000 investment, NPV \$42,000), O (\$100,000 investment, NPV \$22,000). Recommend which to fund.

**Model Answer:**
1. **Concept:** Under capital rationing, rank by Profitability Index, not raw NPV, since PI measures value created per dollar invested.
2. **Case Evidence:** $PI_M = 1 + 30{,}000/120{,}000 = 1.25$; $PI_N = 1 + 42{,}000/150{,}000 = 1.28$; $PI_O = 1 + 22{,}000/100{,}000 = 1.22$.
3. **Analysis:** Ranked by PI: N (1.28) > M (1.25) > O (1.22). N+M = \$270,000 (over budget). N+O = \$250,000 exactly, NPV = \$64,000. M+O = \$220,000, NPV = \$52,000 (\$30,000 unused).
4. **Implication:** N + O fits the budget exactly and delivers the highest achievable combined NPV of the feasible bundles.
5. **Recommendation:** Fund Project N and Project O — combined NPV of \$64,000, the best outcome within the \$250,000 cap.

## Consolidated Trap Checklist (Weeks 1–3)

1. **Same Time, Same Terms:** Never add/compare cash flows at different points in time without moving them to a common date first.
2. **Direction Check:** Forward → bigger (FV); backward → smaller (PV). Wrong direction = wrong formula.
3. **Decimals, Not Percentages:** Convert 9% to 0.09 before plugging in.
4. **Rate/Period Matching:** Monthly payments need monthly rate and $n$ (APR/12, years × 12).
5. **EAR Before Comparing:** Never compare APRs at different compounding frequencies directly — convert both to EAR.
6. **Annuity Due Keyword Scan:** "In advance," "at the start of," "immediately" → Annuity Due → extra $\times(1+r)$.
7. **NPV Rules the Roost:** For mutually exclusive projects, always choose highest NPV — never highest IRR or shortest Payback.
8. **Unequal Lives → Use EAA:** Never compare raw NPVs across different lifespans; annualise first.
9. **Limited Budget → Rank by PI:** A capital constraint across independent projects = switch from NPV to PI ranking.
10. **$CF_0$ is Negative:** The initial outlay is a cash *outflow* in your NPV sum.

## One-Page Formula Cheat Sheet (Weeks 1–3)

**Single Cash Flows**
- Future Value: $FV = PV(1+r)^n$
- Present Value: $PV = FV / (1+r)^n$

**Interest Rate Conversion**
- EAR: $EAR = (1 + APR/m)^m - 1$

**Cash Flow Streams**
- Ordinary Annuity PV: $PV = C \times \left[\dfrac{1}{r} - \dfrac{1}{r(1+r)^n}\right]$
- Annuity Due PV: $PV_{due} = PV_{ordinary} \times (1+r)$
- Perpetuity: $PV = C/r$
- Growing Perpetuity: $PV = C_1/(r-g)$

**Investment Decisions**
- NPV: $NPV = CF_0 + \sum \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$ — accept if $NPV > 0$; pick highest NPV for mutually exclusive projects
- Profitability Index: $PI = PV_{future\ CF} / Initial\ Investment$ — use to rank under capital rationing
- Equivalent Annual Annuity: $EAA = NPV / Annuity\ Factor$ — use to compare projects of different lifespans
- Payback Period: years to recover $CF_0$ from cumulative cash flows — liquidity check only, ignores time value of money
