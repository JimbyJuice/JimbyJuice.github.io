# Ember & Bloom — Frameworks Applied (Weeks 4, 5, 7, 10)

For each framework: what it is, then the specific case evidence, then how strongly the case supports it (Strong / Potential / Not supported — so you don't invent connections that aren't there).

---

# WEEK 4 — Customers as a Source of Value

## 1. STP (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)
**Strongly supported** — this is the best-evidenced framework in the case.

**Segmentation** (given explicitly, with %s):
- Young Adults 18–30 (30% of sales) — price-sensitive, social-media driven, renters, buy on promotion
- Homeowners/Professionals 30–50 (35%) — premium, repeat purchasers, low price sensitivity
- Gift Buyers (20%) — packaging/presentation-led
- Hospitality & Corporate (15%) — bulk, customised, values reliability

**Targeting:** the case doesn't state an explicit target, so this is where you argue. Homeowners/Professionals is the strongest case (largest revenue share, least price-sensitive, best fit with premium/Australian-made positioning). Hospitality/Corporate is a defensible alternative if you're arguing for stable, less-seasonal revenue (ties to the Sales Manager's position).

**Positioning:** "premium, Australian-made, natural, sustainable" — check this against evidence: 68% prefer AU-made at comparable price, 74% rate quality over price, 63% consider sustainability. Positioning is reasonably well supported by the data, which is itself worth noting (i.e. the brand story matches what customers say they want).

## 2. Ansoff Matrix
**Strongly supported** via the Smart Home Fragrance System.

- Existing market (same broad customer base, still home fragrance) + new product (app-controlled electric diffuser, tech-enabled) → most defensibly **Product Development**, arguable case for **Diversification** if you emphasise it's a genuinely new product category (consumer electronics/IoT) requiring new capabilities (electronic assembly).
- Risk discussion: Product Development carries moderate risk (new capability, same customer trust); Diversification would carry higher risk. The case flags the tension itself — "differentiate from traditional candle manufacturers" vs "distract from core brand identity."

## 3. Value Proposition Canvas
**Potentially applicable** — the case gives you raw material but you have to build the canvas yourself.

- Customer jobs: decorate home, relax/de-stress, give a gift, furnish a business space
- Customer pains: cheap imports feel low-quality; some concern about sustainability/packaging waste; price sensitivity in a cost-of-living squeeze
- Customer gains: natural ingredients, supporting local manufacturing, gifting presentation, tech convenience (Smart System)
- Pain relievers: soy/local wax, refillable containers, jar return discount program
- Gain creators: premium fragrance ranges, customisable labels, app-controlled scheduling

## 4. Types of Customer Value (Smith & Colgate 2007)
**Strongly supported** across the product range.

| Category | Case evidence |
|---|---|
| Functional | Fragrance performance (soy/coconut wax blend improves burn), diffuser scheduling function |
| Experiential | Wellness Collection — relaxation, mindfulness, calming scents |
| Symbolic | Australian-made identity, sustainability signalling, gifting status |
| Cost/sacrifice | Premium price point; refill/jar-return program reduces long-run cost; convenience of auto-reorder cartridges |

## 5. Create Shared Value (CSV)
**Strong** via the sustainability program, but be specific — don't just say "they're sustainable."

- Genuine CSV candidates: solar (40% of factory electricity — reduces cost AND emissions simultaneously), local supplier partnerships (reduces transport emissions AND supports regional economies), glass jar return program (reduces waste AND builds customer loyalty via discount)
- Where it's NOT yet CSV, just cost: recycled glass proposal (+8% raw material cost) is currently framed as a cost trade-off, not yet linked to a revenue or efficiency gain — a good essay point is to argue how it *could* become CSV (e.g., marketed to justify premium pricing to the 63% who care about sustainability)

# WEEK 5 — Delivering Value

## 1. Porter's Value Chain
**Strongly supported** — the case gives concrete detail across most activities.

**Primary activities:**
- **Inbound logistics:** Australian-grown soy wax, essential oils from VIC/TAS/WA suppliers, SA-made glass jars, AU hardwood timber lids — BUT some fragrance compounds and wicks still imported (a genuine vulnerability/cost-inefficiency to flag — reliance on overseas suppliers not yet localised)
- **Operations:** automated wax melting + hand-finishing at Geelong factory, running at 82% capacity (~650,000 units/year) — room to scale before new capex is strictly required, though management is already considering new automated filling equipment
- **Outbound logistics:** distribution via online store, boutique gift retailers, homewares stores, department stores, plus bulk/customised delivery to hospitality and corporate clients
- **Marketing & sales:** heavy skew to Instagram/TikTok + influencers (52% combined budget) — a potential source of competitive advantage (strong digital reach) but also a possible misallocation (see Week 7/marketing-mix critique)
- **Service:** personalisation/customisation for weddings, birthdays, corporate gifts; glass jar return discount program acts as a post-purchase service touchpoint

**Support activities:**
- **Procurement:** deliberate strategy of local-supplier partnerships to cut transport distances — a genuine value-chain differentiator vs import-reliant competitors
- **Technology development:** currently a weakness — fragmented IT systems (separate inventory, manufacturing, online sales, loyalty systems) causing inventory discrepancies and poor cross-channel visibility; proposed $850k ERP is the fix
- **HR management:** 88 employees across manufacturing, design, marketing, sales, logistics, admin — case gives limited detail here, so treat as **potential**, not strong, unless a question specifically raises staffing/training
- **Firm infrastructure:** founder-led (2 industrial designers + 1 marketing exec), privately owned, functional management structure (visible via the four disagreeing managers) — useful if a question asks about internal decision-making or strategic alignment

**Where competitive advantage could be strengthened:** procurement (local sourcing) and service (customisation) are the standout differentiators; technology development is the clearest current weakness.

## 2. Customer Journey (Awareness → Interest → Desire → Action → Retention)
**Strong**, especially the built-in drop-off point.

- **Awareness:** 71% discover new brands via Instagram/TikTok/Pinterest — matches the marketing spend
- **Interest/Desire:** driven by fragrance (73% say most important factor), Australian-made appeal (79%), sustainable packaging appeal (61%)
- **Action:** purchase via online store, retail, or department stores
- **Retention:** this is the weak link — only **29% of customers have used the refillable jar program**, despite 61% saying sustainable packaging attracts them. That's a stated-preference vs actual-behaviour gap at the retention/post-purchase stage. Good evidence for a "identify the journey weakness and recommend a fix" question (e.g., better communication of the program at point of sale, simplifying the return process, incentivising more strongly).

## 3. Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)
**Potential**, not strongly evidenced with numbers, but conceptually applicable.

- Homeowners/Professionals segment (35% of sales, repeat purchasers, low price sensitivity) is the clearest high-CLV segment — worth prioritising retention investment here
- Hospitality/Corporate clients also plausible high-CLV given recurring bulk orders and branding relationships
- Young Adults segment likely lower CLV per customer (promotion-driven, price-sensitive) but potentially high volume/acquisition value

## 4. Distribution Channels
**Strongly supported** — multiple channel types explicitly named.

- **Direct:** online store (full control, direct customer data, but limited reach)
- **Indirect:** boutique retailers, homewares stores, department stores (greater reach/scale, less control over customer experience and data)
- Trade-off worth discussing: expanding into Hospitality/Corporate (per the Sales Manager's proposal) would likely require more direct B2B relationship management rather than retail-style indirect distribution — different channel economics and control requirements

## 5. Churn
**Not strongly supported** — the case gives no explicit churn/retention rate data. Treat this as background theory only unless a question explicitly invokes it; don't force it onto specific numbers that aren't there.

# WEEK 7 — Pricing & CVP Analysis

## 1. Pricing Tripod (Costs / Competitors / Customer Value)
**Strong**, best applied to the Smart Home Fragrance System ($170 price).

- **Cost floor:** variable cost $96/unit — price must clear this to generate any contribution
- **Competitor context:** cheap paraffin imports at the low end, international luxury brands at the high end — $170 positions clearly toward the premium end, consistent with brand identity
- **Customer value ceiling:** 74% rate quality over price for premium candles, and the product bundles convenience (app control, auto-reorder) which may justify a price premium beyond a standard candle — but also introduces new competitors (smart home device makers) not previously in Ember & Bloom's competitive set

## 2. Variable vs Fixed Costs
**Strong**, explicit numbers given for the new product line.

- Variable: $96/unit manufacturing cost
- Fixed: $920,000/year additional operating costs (plus the $3.1m capex, which is not an annual fixed operating cost but an upfront investment — don't conflate the two in a CVP calc)

## 3. Contribution Margin
CM = $170 − $96 = **$74/unit**

## 4. Break-Even Point
BEP = $920,000 / $74 = **≈ 12,432 units** — well below the 45,000-unit forecast, meaning the forecast clears break-even by a wide margin (good evidence for a viability discussion)

## 5. Multi-Product CVP / Sales Mix
**Potential**, not directly evidenced with numbers across the whole range, but conceptually applicable — the four product ranges (Signature Soy, Native Botanicals, Wellness, Seasonal) almost certainly have different margins (Native Botanicals is explicitly stated as higher-margin/premium), so a sales-mix shift toward Signature Soy (largest, presumably lower-margin) vs Native Botanicals would change the firm's overall weighted CM. No hard numbers given for this, so use it conceptually if a question invites it.

## 6. Target Profit
**Strong**, using the Smart Home Fragrance System numbers as your base for practice — the case itself doesn't state a target profit figure, but gives you everything needed (CM, fixed costs) to build target-profit scenarios exactly like your practice exam questions.

## 7. Pricing Objectives
The $170 pricing decision reads primarily as a **revenue/profit** objective combined with reinforcing premium **positioning** — not a patronage/market-share play, since it's priced well above mass-market imports.

## 8. Value Analysis (VA / NVA)
**Potential.** No explicit NVA activity is called out in the case (unlike your Set B practice exam's carved-chair example), but candidates to reason through:
- Hand-finishing on soy candles — likely VA if it's part of the "handcrafted premium" story customers pay for
- Fragmented IT systems causing inventory discrepancies — arguably NVA (rework, discrepancy correction) until the ERP fixes it
- If a real exam question gives you a specific activity, apply the VA/NVA test: does it directly contribute to what the customer perceives as valuable, or does it just consume resources?

# WEEK 10 — Performance Measurement

The case does **not** hand you a ready-made bad KPI the way the Daroo case study did — so the exam is more likely to ask you to **propose and defend** a measure, or apply the concepts abstractly to a decision in the case (e.g., ERP investment, sustainability program). Treat everything below as **potential**, ready to apply if the actual exam gives you a specific measure.

## 1. CSF vs KPI
If asked to build the hierarchy for, say, the sustainability strategy:
- Strategic objective: reinforce premium, sustainable brand positioning
- CSF: genuinely reduce environmental footprint without eroding margins
- KPI candidates: % of packaging returned/reused, % renewable energy (currently 40% solar — trackable), variable cost per unit avoided through local sourcing

## 2. Characteristics of Effective Measures (all 8)
Practice applying these to plausible case-based measures, e.g. "% of factory electricity from solar" (objective, accurate, but maybe not fully **functional** if the real goal is total emissions reduction rather than just electricity source) or "number of refillable jars sold" (doesn't capture *why* uptake is only 29% — weak on **sensitivity** to the underlying behavioural driver).

## 3. Financial vs Non-Financial
- Financial: revenue ($21m), net profit ($1.47m), gross margin — all given
- Non-financial candidates: customer satisfaction (not directly measured in case, but survey data is a proxy), factory capacity utilisation (82%, an operational non-financial measure), refillable program participation (29%)

## 4. Quantitative vs Qualitative
Most case data is quantitative (survey %, financials, capacity). Qualitative elements: the Sustainability Manager's and Finance Manager's differing judgements are essentially qualitative arguments you'd need to weigh against the quantitative evidence.

## 5. Internal vs External
- Internal: factory capacity (82%), IT system fragmentation, inventory discrepancies
- External: customer survey results, competitor landscape, market growth data

## 6. Six Capitals
Good integration point across the whole case:
- **Financial:** $21m revenue, $1.47m net profit
- **Manufactured:** Geelong factory, automated equipment, proposed new filling equipment
- **Intellectual:** design capability (founders are industrial designers), brand/IP in fragrance formulations
- **Human:** 88 employees across functions — limited detail, but relevant if training/capability questions arise
- **Social & relational:** local supplier partnerships, community-facing sustainability programs, retailer relationships
- **Natural:** soy wax sourcing, solar energy, glass jar recycling — but also energy-intensive glass manufacturing and reliance on imported inputs, which is where natural capital could be eroded even while financial capital grows (classic "one capital up, another down" essay point — e.g., if the company pursues aggressive short-term revenue growth by ramping cheap imports rather than local materials)

## 7. Activity vs Outcome Measures
If a question proposes something like "number of sustainability initiatives launched" — that's an **activity** measure. A better **outcome** measure would be something like % reduction in packaging waste sent to landfill, or measured customer perception shift on sustainability (tied to the 63% baseline in the survey).

## 8. Dysfunctional Behaviour / Gaming
Speculative but plausible: if staff are measured purely on **units produced** near 82% capacity, they might push volume at the expense of quality/hand-finishing standards that underpin the premium brand — worth having as a ready example if a question asks you to imagine a poorly designed KPI and its consequences.

## 9. Stakeholder Alignment
The case explicitly gives you competing internal stakeholders (Marketing, Sales, Product Development, Finance, Sustainability managers) plus external stakeholders (customers across 4 segments, retail partners, suppliers) — useful if a question asks you to design a "balanced" measurement system rather than one that only serves shareholders.
