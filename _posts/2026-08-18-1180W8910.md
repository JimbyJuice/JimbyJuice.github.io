# PART 1 — WEEK 8: FINANCIAL REPORTING & PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS
*(Note: Per your sources, this week focuses on Estimating the Financial Value of Organisations)*

## 1.1 The Valuation Principle
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
In the real world, the "fair price" of anything that generates money—like a rental property, a bond, or a share—is simply the total of all the money it will give you in the future, adjusted back to what that money is worth *today*. If you know the future cash flows and the "riskiness" (discount rate), you can calculate the exact price you should pay.

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **The Model:** Intrinsic Value (\\(P_0\\)) = Present Value (PV) of expected future cash flows.
*   **Worked Example (Bond Pricing):**
    Daroo issues a bond: Face Value (\\(F\\)) = \$100,000, 6% annual coupon (\\(c\\)) paid semi-annually, 4-year maturity (\\(n\\)), and YTM (\\(r\\)) = 7.2%.
    *   Step 1: Adjust for semi-annual. \\(n = 4 \times 2 = 8\\) periods. \\(r = 7.2\% / 2 = 3.6\%\\). \\(C = (6\% / 2) \times \$100,000 = \$3,000\\).
    *   Step 2: Apply Bond Formula:
    \\[ P_0 = C \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}{r} \right] + \frac{F}{(1 + r)^n} \\]
    *   Step 3: Calculation:
    \\[ P_0 = 3,000 \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.036)^{-8}}{0.036} \right] + \frac{100,000}{(1.036)^8} = \$95,893 \\].
*   **Decision Rule:** If the Coupon Rate < YTM, the bond trades at a **discount** (below face value). If Coupon Rate > YTM, it trades at a **premium**. If they're equal, it trades at **par**.
*   **Second Worked Example (Premium Bond, for contrast):**
    Same Daroo bond but YTM falls to 5.0% (below the 6% coupon). \\(r = 2.5\%\\) per period, \\(C = \$3,000\\), \\(n = 8\\).
    \\[ P_0 = 3,000 \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.025)^{-8}}{0.025} \right] + \frac{100,000}{(1.025)^8} \approx \$103,556 \\]
    Because the coupon (6%) now beats the market rate (5%), investors pay *more* than face value to lock in the higher coupon — confirming the decision rule above.

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **Timing Mismatch:** Students often forget to divide the annual YTM by 2 for semi-annual bonds or forget to double the number of years to get the number of periods (\\(n\\)).
*   **Face Value vs. Price:** \\(F\\) is what you get back at the end; \\(P_0\\) is what you pay now. Do not confuse them in the formula.

## 1.2 Dividend Discount Models (DDM)
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
A share of stock is just a claim on future dividends. If a company stays the same size forever, we use a simple average. If it grows, we have to account for that compounding growth. Valuation is always **forward-looking**—we don't care about the dividend the company *just* paid; we care about the next one.

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **Constant Dividend:** \\[ P_0 = \frac{D}{r} \\]
*   **Constant Growth (Gordon Growth Model):** \\[ P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r_e - g} \\]
    *(Where \\(D_1 = D_0 \times (1+g)\\))*.
*   **Worked Example:** Daroo just paid \\(D_0 = \$2\\). Growth \\(g = 4\%\\). Required return \\(r = 8\%\\).
    *   Step 1: Find \\(D_1 = 2 \times 1.04 = \$2.08\\).
    *   Step 2: \\[ P_0 = \frac{2.08}{0.08 - 0.04} = \$52 \\].
*   **NEW — Two-Stage (Supernormal) Growth Model:** Many exam questions give a firm a few years of high growth before it "settles down" to a stable rate. The trick is to value the two stages separately, then add them together.
    1.  **Stage 1:** Forecast each dividend individually during the high-growth years and discount each back to today at \\(r_e\\).
    2.  **Stage 2:** At the end of the high-growth period (year \\(t\\)), apply the Gordon Growth Model to find the *terminal value* — this gives you the value of all dividends from year \\(t+1\\) onward, but the answer lands at *time \\(t\\)*, not time 0.
    3.  **Combine:** Discount the terminal value back to today (it's still \\(t\\) periods away) and add it to the Stage 1 present values.
    *   **Worked Example:** Daroo just paid \\(D_0 = \$3\\). It will grow at a supernormal 20% for 2 years, then settle to a stable 5% forever. \\(r_e = 11\%\\).
        *   \\(D_1 = 3 \times 1.20 = \$3.60\\); \\(D_2 = 3.60 \times 1.20 = \$4.32\\); \\(D_3 = 4.32 \times 1.05 = \$4.536\\) (first stable dividend).
        *   \\(PV(D_1) = 3.60 / 1.11 = \$3.243\\); \\(PV(D_2) = 4.32 / 1.11^2 = \$3.506\\).
        *   Terminal Value at year 2: \\(TV_2 = D_3 / (r_e - g) = 4.536 / (0.11 - 0.05) = \$75.60\\).
        *   \\(PV(TV_2) = 75.60 / 1.11^2 = \$61.35\\).
        *   \\(P_0 = 3.243 + 3.506 + 61.35 = \$68.10\\).

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **\\(D_0\\) vs. \\(D_1\\):** The most common mistake is putting \\(D_0\\) (the dividend just paid) directly into the numerator. You MUST grow it by \\((1+g)\\) first to get \\(D_1\\).
*   **Growth Sensitivity:** A small change in \\(g\\) leads to a massive change in \\(P_0\\). If \\(g \ge r\\), the formula breaks (math error), meaning the stock would theoretically be worth "infinite" money.
*   **Two-Stage Timing Trap:** The terminal value formula always spits out a value **one year before** the first stable dividend, discounted back to *that* year — not to today. Forgetting the second discounting step (back to time 0) is the single biggest mark-loser on this question type.

## 1.3 Payout Policy and Reinvestment
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
A CEO has two choices with profit: give it to shareholders (Dividends) or keep it to buy more machines/tech (Retention). Reinvesting only helps shareholders if the company can earn a higher return on that money (\\(ROI\\)) than the shareholders could get themselves in the market (\\(r\\)).

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **Sustainable Growth Formula:** \\[ g = Retention\ Rate\ (RR) \times ROI \\].
*   **Value Creation Logic:**
    *   If \\(ROI > r\\): Reinvesting **increases** share price (Value Created).
    *   If \\(ROI < r\\): Reinvesting **decreases** share price (Value Destroyed).
    *   If \\(ROI = r\\): Payout policy is irrelevant to share price (Modigliani–Miller dividend irrelevance intuition).

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **The "Growth is Always Good" Fallacy:** Examiners love to ask if a company should retain 100% of earnings. The answer is **NO** if the \\(ROI\\) is lower than the cost of capital (\\(r\\)). Growth for the sake of growth can destroy firm value.

## 1.4 NEW — Financial Statement Ratio Analysis
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
A single number (e.g. "profit was \$5m") tells you almost nothing on its own — you need to compare it to something (revenue, assets, prior years, competitors) to know if it's *good*. Ratios turn raw financial statement figures into comparable, meaningful signals grouped into four families: can the firm pay its short-term bills (liquidity), is it profitable, how efficiently does it use its assets, and how much debt risk is it carrying (gearing/leverage).

**Hero (The Four Families, Formulas & a Worked Example):**

| Family | Ratio | Formula | What it tells you |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Liquidity** | Current Ratio | \\(\frac{Current\ Assets}{Current\ Liabilities}\\) | Can short-term assets cover short-term debts? |
| **Liquidity** | Quick Ratio | \\(\frac{Current\ Assets - Inventory}{Current\ Liabilities}\\) | Same, but excludes hard-to-sell inventory |
| **Profitability** | Net Profit Margin | \\(\frac{Net\ Profit}{Revenue}\\) | What % of sales becomes profit |
| **Profitability** | Return on Equity (ROE) | \\(\frac{Net\ Profit}{Shareholders'\ Equity}\\) | Return generated on owners' money |
| **Efficiency** | Asset Turnover | \\(\frac{Revenue}{Total\ Assets}\\) | How much sales each dollar of assets generates |
| **Efficiency** | Inventory Turnover | \\(\frac{COGS}{Average\ Inventory}\\) | How many times inventory is sold and replaced per year |
| **Gearing** | Debt-to-Equity | \\(\frac{Total\ Debt}{Total\ Equity}\\) | How much the firm relies on debt vs. owner funding |
| **Gearing** | Interest Coverage | \\(\frac{EBIT}{Interest\ Expense}\\) | How comfortably profit covers interest payments |

*   **Worked Example:** Daroo reports Current Assets \$40m, Current Liabilities \$25m, Inventory \$10m, Net Profit \$6m, Revenue \$60m, Total Assets \$120m, Total Equity \$70m, Total Debt \$50m, EBIT \$9m, Interest Expense \$2m.
    *   Current Ratio \\(= 40/25 = 1.6\\) (healthy — assets cover liabilities 1.6×).
    *   Quick Ratio \\(= (40-10)/25 = 1.2\\) (still comfortably above 1).
    *   Net Profit Margin \\(= 6/60 = 10\%\\).
    *   ROE \\(= 6/70 = 8.6\%\\).
    *   Debt-to-Equity \\(= 50/70 = 0.71\\) (moderate gearing).
    *   Interest Coverage \\(= 9/2 = 4.5\times\\) (comfortable — profit covers interest 4.5 times over).

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **Ratios in Isolation:** A single ratio means nothing without a benchmark — compare to (a) the firm's own prior years (trend), (b) industry peers, or (c) a stated target. Examiners award marks for the *comparison*, not just the number.
*   **Current vs. Quick Ratio Confusion:** Forgetting to strip out inventory in the Quick Ratio is a very common slip, especially for inventory-heavy firms (retailers, harvesters) where the two ratios diverge a lot.
*   **Profit ≠ Cash:** A firm can be profitable (good margin) but still illiquid (bad current ratio) if profit is tied up in receivables or inventory. Don't assume one implies the other.

# PART 2 — WEEK 9: TIME VALUE OF MONEY & CAPITAL BUDGETING
*(Note: Per your sources, this week focuses on Valuing Organisations Using Risk and Return)*

## 2.1 The Nature of Risk
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
Risk is not "losing money"—risk is **uncertainty**. In finance, we split risk into two buckets: (1) things that only affect one company (Idiosyncratic) and (2) things that affect the whole world (Systematic). Since you can get rid of idiosyncratic risk by just buying 30 different stocks (Diversification), the market refuses to pay you for taking it.

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **Risk Comparison:**

| Type of Risk | Definition | Diversifiable? | Market Reward? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Idiosyncratic** | Firm-specific (e.g., a strike at Daroo) | Yes | No |
| **Systematic** | Market-wide (e.g., inflation/recession) | No | **Yes** |

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **Total Risk vs. Systematic Risk:** A start-up might have massive "total risk," but if it's all idiosyncratic, its required return might be lower than expected. The market only prices **Beta (\\(\beta\\))**, which measures sensitivity to the *market*.

## 2.2 Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
CAPM is a "pricing machine." You plug in the "safe" return (bank interest/govt bonds) and add a "bonus" (risk premium) based on how much the stock swings when the market moves.

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **The Formula:** \\[ r_E = r_f + \beta \times (E[r_M] - r_f) \\].
    *   \\(r_f\\): Risk-free rate.
    *   \\(\beta\\): Equity Beta.
    *   \\(E[r_M] - r_f\\): Market Risk Premium (MRP).
*   **Worked Example (Daroo):** \\(r_f = 4.2\%\\), \\(\beta = 1.50\\), \\(MRP = 6.25\%\\).
    *   \\[ r_E = 0.042 + 1.50 \times (0.0625) = 13.575\% \\].
*   **NEW — Reading the Security Market Line (SML):** Plot \\(\beta\\) on the x-axis and required return on the y-axis; CAPM is just the equation of a straight line starting at \\(r_f\\) (where \\(\beta=0\\)) with slope equal to the MRP. A stock with \\(\beta=0\\) should earn exactly \\(r_f\\); a stock with \\(\beta=1\\) should earn exactly the market return \\(E[r_M]\\).

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **MRP vs. Market Return:** Read carefully! If the exam says "The Expected Market **Return** is 10%," you must subtract \\(r_f\\). If it says "The Market Risk **Premium** is 6%," that is already the \\((E[r_M] - r_f)\\) term. Do not subtract \\(r_f\\) twice!.

## 2.3 Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
Companies are usually funded by a mix of bank loans (Debt) and owner money (Equity). WACC is the "average" cost of all that money. Crucially, debt is "cheaper" because interest payments reduce the company's tax bill (Tax Shield).

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **The Formula:** \\[ WACC = w_E r_E + w_P r_P + w_D r_D (1 - T_C) \\].
*   **Worked Example (Step-by-Step):**
    1.  **Find Market Values:** Equity (\\(E\\)) = \\(\$12.50 \times 10m = \$125m\\). Debt (\\(D\\)) = \\(\$250m \times 80\% = \$200m\\). Total Value = \\(\$325m\\) (assuming no preferred).
    2.  **Find Weights:** \\(w_E = 125/325 = 0.385\\). \\(w_D = 200/325 = 0.615\\).
    3.  **Adjust Debt for Tax:** If \\(r_D = 6.5\%\\) and Tax = 30%, After-tax cost = \\(6.5\% \times (1 - 0.30) = 4.55\%\\).
    4.  **Final Blend:** \\[ WACC = (0.385 \times r_E) + (0.615 \times 4.55\%) \\].
*   **NEW — Adding a Preferred Stock Tranche:** Suppose Daroo also has Preferred Stock worth \$25m paying a fixed dividend with \\(r_P = 5.8\%\\). Total Value now = \$125m + \$25m + \$200m = \$350m.
    *   \\(w_E = 125/350 = 0.357\\), \\(w_P = 25/350 = 0.071\\), \\(w_D = 200/350 = 0.571\\).
    *   \\(WACC = (0.357 \times r_E) + (0.071 \times 5.8\%) + (0.571 \times 4.55\%)\\). Note preferred dividends, like ordinary dividends, get **no** tax shield.

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **The Book Value Trap:** NEVER use "Book Value" or "Face Value" to calculate weights (\\(w_E, w_D\\)) unless market values are impossible to find. WACC must reflect today's market prices.
*   **Tax Shield on Equity:** There is **NO** tax shield for equity (dividends are not tax-deductible). Never multiply \\(r_E\\) by \\((1-T_C)\\).

## 2.4 NEW — Capital Budgeting Decision Rules (NPV, IRR, Payback)
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
Once you know your discount rate (WACC), you can decide whether a *project* — not just a whole company — is worth doing. The core idea never changes: bring every cash flow the project produces back to today's dollars, and see if what you get back is worth more than what you put in. NPV is the gold standard because it directly measures dollars of value created; IRR and Payback are useful cross-checks but each has blind spots.

**Hero (Models, Formulas & Worked Examples):**
*   **Net Present Value (NPV):** \\[ NPV = -C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} \\]
    **Decision Rule:** Accept if \\(NPV > 0\\) (project adds value); reject if \\(NPV < 0\\). Between mutually exclusive projects, pick the **higher** NPV.
*   **Internal Rate of Return (IRR):** the discount rate that makes \\(NPV = 0\\).
    **Decision Rule:** Accept if \\(IRR > r\\) (the required/hurdle rate, usually WACC).
*   **Payback Period:** the number of years for cumulative cash flows to recover the initial investment. Ignores the time value of money and any cash flows after payback — use only as a quick liquidity/risk screen, never as the primary decision tool.
*   **Worked Example:** Daroo invests \\(C_0 = \$500,000\\) in a machine expected to generate \\(\$180,000\\) per year for 4 years. \\(r = 10\%\\) (the project's WACC).
    *   Step 1 — NPV:
    \\[ NPV = -500,000 + \frac{180,000}{1.10} + \frac{180,000}{1.10^2} + \frac{180,000}{1.10^3} + \frac{180,000}{1.10^4} \\]
    \\[ NPV = -500,000 + 570,750 \approx \$70,750 \\]
    Since \\(NPV > 0\\), **accept** the project — it adds roughly \$70,750 of value in today's dollars.
    *   Step 2 — Payback: \$500,000 / \$180,000 ≈ 2.8 years (cumulative cash flows cross \$500,000 partway through year 3).
    *   Step 3 — IRR (intuition, not hand-calculated): since NPV is positive at 10%, IRR must be *above* 10% — the exam typically wants you to state this relationship rather than solve IRR by hand.

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **NPV vs. IRR Conflicts:** For mutually exclusive projects of different sizes or cash flow timing, NPV and IRR can disagree — **always follow NPV**, since it measures dollar value created, while IRR (a percentage) can be misleading when comparing projects of different scale.
*   **Multiple IRRs:** If a project's cash flows change sign more than once (e.g. a large cost in a later year, like a mine rehabilitation), there can be more than one IRR, making the rule unreliable — NPV doesn't have this problem.
*   **Ignoring Timing in Payback:** Two projects can have the identical payback period but very different NPVs if one delivers its cash earlier — Payback treats a dollar in year 1 the same as a dollar in year 4, which is exactly the mistake the whole course is trying to train you out of.
*   **Sunk Costs:** Only include *incremental* cash flows caused by the decision. Money already spent (market research, a feasibility study) is a sunk cost and must be **excluded** from the NPV calculation even if it's mentioned in the case.

# PART 3 — WEEK 10: STRATEGY, ORGANISATIONAL DESIGN & MANAGING CHANGE
*(Note: Per your sources, this week focuses on Performance Measurement)*

## 3.1 Integrated Reporting (IR) Framework
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
Traditional accounting only looks at cash. Integrated Reporting argues that a company's "value" is stored in six different buckets (Capitals). If you make a huge profit (Financial Capital) but destroy your reputation (Social Capital) or pollute the river (Natural Capital), you haven't actually created value—you've just moved it from one bucket to another.

**Hero (The 6 Capitals):**

| Capital | Meaning in Plain English | KPI Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Financial** | The actual cash and funding | ROI, Earnings per share |
| **Manufactured** | Physical stuff (buildings, inventory) | Capacity utilisation rate |
| **Intellectual** | Systems, software, patents, and "know-how" | Number of patents filed |
| **Human** | Skills, capabilities, and motivation of staff | Employee retention rate |
| **Social/Relational** | Relationships with customers and community | Customer satisfaction score |
| **Natural** | Environmental resources (air, water, land) | Carbon emissions (Scope 1 & 2) |

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **Human vs. Social Capital:** Students often mix these up. **Human** is *inside* the firm (employees' brains/skills). **Social** is *between* the firm and the outside world (trust with Traditional Custodians or local neighbors).

## 3.2 Developing Effective KPIs
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
"What is measured is managed." If you measure the wrong thing, you get the wrong behavior. A good measure must be sensitive (changes when performance changes) and objective (the same regardless of who measures it).

**Hero (The Measurement Hierarchy):**
1.  **Strategic Objective:** "Be the safest harvester in Australia".
2.  **Critical Success Factor (CSF):** "Zero accidents in processing plants".
3.  **Key Performance Indicator (KPI):** "Number of lost-time injuries per 100,000 hours".

**ASCII Comparison of KPI Types:**
*   **Quantitative (Numbers)** vs. **Qualitative (Narratives)**.
*   **Internal (Processes)** vs. **External (Stakeholder impact)**.
*   **Financial (Accounting \$)** vs. **Non-Financial (Operational)**.

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **Activity vs. Outcome:** This is the #1 way to lose marks. An **Activity** is "held 5 safety meetings." An **Outcome** is "10% reduction in injuries." ALWAYS propose Outcome measures in the exam.

## 3.3 Dysfunctional Consequences (Gamesmanship)
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
People are smart—if you reward them for one number, they will find "tricks" to make that number look good, even if it hurts the company. This is called "gaming the system".

**Hero (Types of Gaming):**
*   **Budgetary Slack:** Managers ask for more money than they need so they can "beat" their budget easily.
*   **Data Manipulation:** Delaying an expense until next month just to hit this month's profit target.
*   **Operational Delays:** Stopping work to fill out forms just because "form completion" is a KPI.

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **Ignoring Trade-offs:** There is no "perfect" measure. If you increase the speed of a production line (Efficiency KPI), you will likely decrease Quality. You must mention these trade-offs in your analysis.

## 3.4 NEW — The Balanced Scorecard (BSC)
**Zero (Intuitive Foundation):**
A single KPI is a keyhole view of the business. The Balanced Scorecard forces managers to look through four keyholes at once so that success in one area (e.g. Financial) can't hide failure in another (e.g. Customer or Learning). It also builds a chain of cause-and-effect: investing in people and systems today should show up as better internal processes, which shows up as happier customers, which eventually shows up as better financial results.

**Hero (The Four Perspectives):**

| Perspective | Core Question | Example Measure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Financial** | How do we look to shareholders? | ROI, revenue growth |
| **Customer** | How do customers see us? | Customer satisfaction, market share |
| **Internal Business Process** | What must we excel at? | Defect rate, cycle time |
| **Learning & Growth** | Can we keep improving and creating value? | Employee training hours, staff turnover |

*   **The Causal Chain (exam-favourite diagram):** \\[ Learning\ \&\ Growth \rightarrow Internal\ Process \rightarrow Customer \rightarrow Financial \\]
    Improving staff skills (Learning & Growth) → reduces defects (Internal Process) → improves satisfaction (Customer) → drives revenue (Financial).

**Trap (Exam Pitfalls):**
*   **BSC vs. IR's 6 Capitals — don't conflate them.** The Balanced Scorecard has **4** perspectives and is primarily an internal *management/strategy execution* tool. Integrated Reporting has **6** capitals and is primarily an external *stakeholder communication* tool. Examiners sometimes ask you to distinguish the two directly.
*   **Treating it as Financial-only:** The whole point of the BSC is to stop managers over-weighting the Financial perspective just because it's the easiest to measure — always propose at least one non-financial measure when answering a BSC question.

# PART 4 — PRACTICE EXAM QUESTIONS & MODEL RESPONSES

### Case Study 1: The Reinvestment Dilemma (Finance Focus)
**Question (7 Marks):** Daroo is currently a mature firm with an \\(EPS_1\\) of \$7.75. Shareholders require a 12% return (\\(r_e\\)). The CFO is considering two options: (A) Pay out 100% of earnings as dividends, or (B) Retain 35% of earnings to invest in a new facility with an expected ROI of 18%.
**Requirement:** Calculate the share price under both options and recommend a course of action.

**Model Answer:**
1.  **Concept:** The value of a share depends on the present value of future dividends, which is driven by the growth rate (\\(g = RR \times ROI\\)).
2.  **Case Evidence:** Option A: \\(RR = 0\\), \\(g = 0\\), \\(D_1 = \$7.75\\). Option B: \\(RR = 0.35\\), \\(ROI = 0.18\\), \\(r_e = 0.12\\).
3.  **Analysis:**
    *   **Option A Price:** \\(P_0 = \$7.75 / 0.12 = \$64.58\\).
    *   **Option B Growth:** \\(g = 0.35 \times 0.18 = 6.3\%\\).
    *   **Option B Dividend:** \\(D_1 = \$7.75 \times (1 - 0.35) = \$5.0375\\).
    *   **Option B Price:** \\(P_0 = 5.0375 / (0.12 - 0.063) = \$88.38\\).
4.  **Implication:** Retaining earnings for the project increases the share price by \$23.80 (\$88.38 - \$64.58). This is because the project's \\(ROI\\) (18%) is significantly higher than the cost of equity (12%).
5.  **Recommendation:** Daroo should adopt Option B as it creates superior shareholder value through profitable reinvestment.

### Case Study 2: Measuring Sustainability (Management Focus)
**Question (8 Marks):** Environmental groups are concerned about Daroo's impact on biodiversity. Daroo's strategic objective is "Sustainable harvesting."
**Requirement:** Propose one specific, effective KPI for this objective and identify a potential dysfunctional consequence.

**Model Answer:**
1.  **Concept:** Performance measurement requires translating a high-level strategic objective into a specific, sensitive, and objective KPI that measures an outcome, not just an activity.
2.  **Case Evidence:** Strategic Objective = Sustainable Harvesting. Stakeholder = Environmental groups.
3.  **Analysis:** A strong KPI would be the "Percentage of harvesting zones showing no decline in local biodiversity species counts over a 12-month period".
4.  **Implication:** This is a **Non-Financial, Quantitative, External** measure. It is sensitive because a single bad harvest would lower the percentage, and it is objective if audited by a third party.
5.  **Recommendation:** Monitor this alongside a "Volume Harvested" KPI to manage the **dysfunctional behavior** where harvesters might hide or fail to report biodiversity loss in order to meet their production quotas (Data Manipulation).

### Case Study 3: NEW — Should Daroo Build the New Plant? (Capital Budgeting Focus)
**Question (8 Marks):** Daroo is evaluating a new processing plant costing \$2,000,000 today. It is expected to generate net cash flows of \$650,000 per year for 5 years. Daroo's WACC (calculated from its capital structure) is 11%.
**Requirement:** Calculate the NPV and Payback period, and recommend whether Daroo should proceed. State one limitation of your recommendation.

**Model Answer:**
1.  **Concept:** A project should be accepted if it creates value for shareholders, measured by whether the present value of its future cash flows exceeds the initial outlay (NPV), discounted at the firm's cost of capital.
2.  **Case Evidence:** \\(C_0 = \$2,000,000\\), \\(C_t = \$650,000\\) for \\(t = 1..5\\), \\(r = 11\%\\).
3.  **Analysis:**
    *   Sum of discounted cash flows \\(\approx \$650,000 \times 3.696 = \$2,402,400\\) (using the 5-year, 11% annuity factor).
    *   \\(NPV = -2,000,000 + 2,402,400 = \$402,400\\).
    *   Payback \\(= 2,000,000 / 650,000 \approx 3.08\\) years.
4.  **Implication:** Since NPV is positive (\$402,400), the plant is expected to add value beyond covering Daroo's cost of capital. Payback of ~3.1 years also seems reasonable relative to the 5-year project life.
5.  **Recommendation:** Proceed with the plant. **Limitation:** Payback ignores cash flows after year 5 and the time value of money, so it should only be used as a secondary liquidity check — the accept decision should rest on the NPV result.

### Case Study 4: NEW — Is Daroo's Liquidity Deteriorating? (Ratio Analysis Focus)
**Question (6 Marks):** Daroo's Current Ratio fell from 2.1 last year to 1.3 this year, while its Net Profit Margin rose from 8% to 11% over the same period.
**Requirement:** Explain what these two trends suggest about Daroo's financial position, and identify one further piece of information you would request before concluding.

**Model Answer:**
1.  **Concept:** Profitability ratios and liquidity ratios measure different things — a firm can be simultaneously more profitable and less liquid, especially if profit growth is being funded by drawing down cash or extending inventory/receivables.
2.  **Case Evidence:** Current Ratio: 2.1 → 1.3. Net Profit Margin: 8% → 11%.
3.  **Analysis:** The rising margin suggests Daroo is becoming more efficient at converting sales into profit. However, the falling Current Ratio (still above the common 1.0 threshold, but trending down sharply) suggests current liabilities are growing faster than current assets — this could reflect increased short-term borrowing, slower collection of receivables, or profits being reinvested in non-current assets rather than held as liquid working capital.
4.  **Implication:** Taken together, Daroo may be pursuing an aggressive growth or reinvestment strategy that is improving profitability at the cost of short-term financial flexibility.
5.  **Recommendation:** Request the **Quick Ratio** and a breakdown of current liabilities (e.g. how much is short-term debt vs. trade payables) to determine whether the liquidity decline is a genuine risk or simply a shift in working capital management.

### Case Study 5: NEW — Designing a Balanced Scorecard for a New Strategy (Management Focus)
**Question (8 Marks):** Daroo has just announced a new strategic priority: "Become the most trusted harvester among Traditional Custodian communities within 3 years."
**Requirement:** Propose one measure for each of the four Balanced Scorecard perspectives that supports this strategy, and briefly justify the Learning & Growth measure.

**Model Answer:**
1.  **Concept:** The Balanced Scorecard translates a single strategic priority into measures across four perspectives so that progress can be tracked holistically rather than through one financial number alone.
2.  **Case Evidence:** Strategic Objective = trust with Traditional Custodian communities within 3 years.
3.  **Analysis (one measure per perspective):**
    *   **Financial:** Value of long-term supply/partnership agreements signed with Traditional Custodian-owned enterprises.
    *   **Customer/Stakeholder:** Community trust score from an independent annual survey of Traditional Custodian representatives.
    *   **Internal Business Process:** Number of harvesting decisions made with documented Traditional Custodian consultation prior to approval.
    *   **Learning & Growth:** Hours of cultural competency training completed per employee per year.
4.  **Implication:** The Learning & Growth measure is the *leading* indicator in the causal chain — better-trained staff should improve the quality of consultation (Internal Process), which should improve trust (Customer), which should ultimately translate into stronger commercial partnerships (Financial).
5.  **Recommendation:** Track all four measures together, since a rise in Financial results alone (without improvement in the Customer trust score) would suggest the strategy is not genuinely succeeding on its own terms.

# PART 6 — NEW: ONE-PAGE FORMULA CHEAT SHEET

**Valuation**
*   Bond Price: \\(P_0 = C \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r} \right] + \frac{F}{(1+r)^n}\\)
*   Constant Dividend: \\(P_0 = D / r\\)
*   Gordon Growth: \\(P_0 = D_1 / (r_e - g)\\), where \\(D_1 = D_0(1+g)\\)
*   Sustainable Growth: \\(g = RR \times ROI\\)

**Risk & Return**
*   CAPM: \\(r_E = r_f + \beta(E[r_M] - r_f)\\)
*   WACC: \\(WACC = w_E r_E + w_P r_P + w_D r_D(1 - T_C)\\)

**Capital Budgeting**
*   NPV: \\(NPV = -C_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}\\) — accept if \\(NPV > 0\\)
*   IRR: rate where \\(NPV = 0\\) — accept if \\(IRR > r\\)
*   Payback: years to recover \\(C_0\\) from cumulative cash flows (secondary check only)

**Ratio Analysis**
*   Current Ratio: \\(CA / CL\\)
*   Quick Ratio: \\((CA - Inventory) / CL\\)
*   Net Profit Margin: \\(Net\ Profit / Revenue\\)
*   ROE: \\(Net\ Profit / Equity\\)
*   Asset Turnover: \\(Revenue / Total\ Assets\\)
*   Debt-to-Equity: \\(Total\ Debt / Total\ Equity\\)
*   Interest Coverage: \\(EBIT / Interest\ Expense\\)

**Performance Measurement**
*   IR's 6 Capitals: Financial, Manufactured, Intellectual, Human, Social/Relational, Natural
*   BSC's 4 Perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Business Process, Learning & Growth
*   Measurement Hierarchy: Strategic Objective → Critical Success Factor → KPI
*   Always favour: **Outcome** measures over **Activity** measures
