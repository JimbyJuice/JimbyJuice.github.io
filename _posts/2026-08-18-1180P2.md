## Q1 — STP (5 marks, 200–250 words)

Using STP, Ember & Bloom should prioritise the **Homeowners and Professionals segment (30–50 years)** as the primary target for the Smart Home Fragrance System. This segment is segmented behaviourally and demographically: aged 30–50, repeat purchasers, low price sensitivity, and currently the largest revenue contributor (35% of sales).

In terms of targeting criteria, this segment scores highly on attractiveness — it is the single largest and most stable revenue base, and its low price sensitivity directly suits a premium-priced $170 product, unlike the price-driven Young Adults segment. Fit with company capabilities is also strong: this segment already values Ember & Bloom's premium, sophisticated, Australian-made positioning (68% of consumers prefer AU-made at comparable prices; 74% rate quality over price for premium candles), so extending that trust into a smart, app-controlled product is a natural brand extension rather than a repositioning risk.

Case evidence supports this: professionals value convenience and quality over novelty for its own sake, meaning features like automated scheduling and refill reminders solve a genuine time-poor lifestyle pain point rather than functioning as a gimmick. Growth potential also exists here, since this segment already purchases repeatedly throughout the year, suggesting a subscription-style cartridge reorder model would be well received. Targeting this segment minimises risk to brand identity while giving the new product its best chance of premium-price acceptance.

## Q2 — CSV (4 marks, 150–200 words)

Shared value (Porter & Kramer, 2011) is created when a firm's policies and operating practices simultaneously enhance its own competitiveness *and* advance social or environmental conditions — distinct from simply donating profits to a cause (CSR), because the value creation and the social benefit occur through the same core business activity.

Ember & Bloom can extend this through its use of **Australian-grown soy wax and local supplier partnerships across Victoria, Tasmania, and Western Australia**. Sourcing locally reduces transport distances and associated emissions (a genuine environmental/social benefit) while simultaneously lowering supply-chain risk and reinforcing the "Australian-made" brand promise that 68% of surveyed consumers say they prefer — directly increasing willingness to pay. This is shared value because the same sourcing decision creates economic value (brand differentiation, premium pricing power) and social value (reduced emissions, support for regional Australian producers) at once, rather than treating sustainability as a separate cost centre. The company could deepen this by transparently communicating supply-chain distances to the 63% of customers who already weigh sustainability in their purchase decision.

## Q3 — Smith & Colgate Value (4 marks, 150–200 words)

**Functional value:** the Native Botanicals Collection uses Australian native essential oils, offering customers a genuinely effective, high-quality fragrance product that reliably performs its core function — scenting a home or space to a premium standard.

**Experiential value:** the premium natural fragrances create sensory enjoyment and a mood-lifting or calming experience in the home, appealing to customers seeking an elevated, indulgent atmosphere rather than a purely functional purchase.

**Symbolic value:** owning and displaying a premium, Australian-made, native-ingredient product signals sophistication, environmental consciousness, and support for local industry — customers derive social meaning and status from the association, particularly relevant given 63% of consumers weigh sustainability in their decisions.

**Cost/sacrifice value:** although priced at a premium, customers justify the cost through perceived quality and longevity, and through minimised effort/risk — buying a trusted, established local brand reduces the perceived risk of disappointment compared to unfamiliar imported alternatives, offsetting the higher price with confidence in the purchase.

## Q4 — Ansoff Matrix (4 marks, 150 words)

The Smart Home Fragrance System is best classified as **Product Development** — a new product introduced to Ember & Bloom's existing market of home fragrance consumers.

This classification is justified because the product still targets the company's established customer base (people seeking home fragrance solutions) rather than an entirely new market; what changes is the product form (app-controlled electric diffuser and cartridge system) rather than the customer.

In terms of risk, Product Development carries moderate risk — higher than Market Penetration (selling existing products to existing customers), since it requires new capabilities in electronic assembly and automated filling that Ember & Bloom has not previously needed. However, it is considerably lower risk than Diversification, because the company is not simultaneously entering an unfamiliar market; it retains its existing brand trust, distribution channels, and customer relationships, reducing the uncertainty typically associated with launching genuinely new products to genuinely new audiences.

## Q5 — Customer Journey (3 marks, 120–150 words)

The drop-off is most likely occurring at the **Retention** stage of the customer journey, not Awareness. The 61% figure shows customers are already aware of and attracted to the *general concept* of sustainable packaging at the point of purchase (Desire/Action) — the gap only appears afterward, in ongoing post-purchase engagement with a specific, recurring program.

This suggests customers buy the product intending to be sustainable, but the refillable jar program itself is not being reinforced or made easy to act on repeatedly. A targeted strategy is to embed the program into the point-of-sale and post-purchase experience — for example, printing simple return instructions directly on jar labels, sending an automated reminder email a few weeks after purchase, or offering an immediate discount code redeemable only through the return process — converting passive attraction into an active, repeated retention behaviour.

## Q6 — CVP Analysis (6 marks)

**a.** CM per unit = $170 − $96 = **$74**

**b.** BEP = $920,000 / $74 = 12,432.4 → **12,433 units** (rounded up, since partial units aren't sellable)

**c.** The forecast of 45,000 units substantially exceeds the break-even point of 12,433 units — a margin of safety of roughly 32,567 units, or about 72% above break-even. This indicates the project is highly viable in its first year: not only does it comfortably cover fixed costs, but the surplus volume (45,000 − 12,433 = 32,567 units × $74 CM ≈ $2.41 million) generates a substantial first-year contribution toward profit, well beyond simply recovering costs.

## Q7 — Target Profit (4 marks)

Pre-tax target profit = After-tax target profit / (1 − tax rate)
= $500,000 / (1 − 0.30) = $500,000 / 0.70 = **$714,285.71**

Units required = (Fixed Costs + Pre-tax Target Profit) / CM per unit
= ($920,000 + $714,285.71) / $74
= $1,634,285.71 / $74
= 22,085.0 → **22,086 units** (rounded up)

*(Note: fixed costs must be added to the pre-tax target profit before dividing by CM — dividing pre-tax profit alone by CM only tells you units needed for that profit in isolation, not units needed to also cover fixed costs.)*

## Q8 — Pricing Tripod (4 marks, 150–200 words)

**Cost floor:** the $170 price sits well above the $96 variable cost per unit, and comfortably covers the $920,000 fixed costs at forecast volume (break-even of only 12,433 units against 45,000 forecast) — so the price clears the cost floor with a wide margin.

**Competitor context:** Ember & Bloom competes against cheap paraffin imports at the low end and international luxury home fragrance brands at the high end. The $170 price positions the product firmly in the premium bracket, consistent with the brand's existing identity — but the Smart Home System also introduces a new competitive set (smart home/diffuser technology products) that a standard candle never faced, and this comparison is not yet addressed by the case.

**Customer value ceiling:** with 74% of consumers rating quality over price for premium candles, and the product adding genuine convenience (scheduling, auto-reorder), there is room to price above a standard candle. Overall, $170 appears appropriate, sitting comfortably above the cost floor and plausibly below the value ceiling for the target segment, provided the product is marketed against premium fragrance competitors rather than mass-market imports.

## Q9 — Performance Measurement (6 marks)

**a. Problems**

*% of electricity from solar panels*: fails the **functional** characteristic, because the underlying goal is reducing the company's *overall* environmental footprint, but this measure only captures one input (electricity source) and ignores transport emissions, raw material sourcing, and waste. It is also low on **sensitivity** — the measure could stay flat or even improve while other environmental impacts worsen, so it doesn't reliably detect genuine change in overall sustainability performance.

*Number of refillable jars sold per month*: also fails **functional**, because it measures a *sales* outcome, not *participation in the return/refill program* — the actual value creation goal. A customer could buy a refillable jar and never return it, and the measure would still look strong. It risks a dysfunctional behaviour: staff could be incentivised to push refill jar *sales* (e.g. through discounting) without any corresponding increase in actual jar returns, inflating the metric while the real sustainability goal goes unmet.

**b. Alternatives**

For overall environmental footprint, use **greenhouse gas emissions per unit produced** — this captures the company's total environmental impact across energy, transport, and materials, giving a genuinely functional and sensitive measure of the stated goal.

For program engagement, use **percentage of sold refillable jars actually returned** — this directly measures participation rather than sales, closing the gap between the stated goal (encouraging *use* of the program) and what is actually being tracked.
