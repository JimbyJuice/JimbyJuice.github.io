# COMM1180 Final Exam — Zero to Hero Study Guide
### Weeks 4 & 5 (Marketing) + Week 7 (Accounting)

This guide moves in three passes for every topic: **Zero** (plain-English foundation) → **Hero** (formulas/frameworks + worked example) → **Trap** (the exact way examiners catch people out). Read it once top to bottom, then use the Traps sections as your final-day checklist.

---

## PART 1 — WEEK 4: FOUNDATIONS OF MARKETING VALUE

### 1.1 Customers as a Source of Value

**Zero:** A business only exists because it sits at the intersection of two things — what customers *need*, and what the firm is *capable* of delivering. Marketing's job is to find and grow that intersection.

**Hero:** Value creation = Customer Needs ∩ Firm Capabilities → Value Proposition → Economic Value (revenue/profit) for the firm and satisfaction for the customer.

**Trap:** Don't describe this as "selling what we're good at." The firm's capability only matters if it maps onto a real customer need — capability alone isn't value.

---

### 1.2 STP: Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning

**Zero:**
- **Segmentation** = slicing the whole market into meaningful groups.
- **Targeting** = picking which group(s) you'll actually serve.
- **Positioning** = deciding how you want to be perceived in that group's mind, relative to competitors.

**Hero — worked distinction:**
> "A firm identifies three customer segments (budget travellers, business travellers, families) and chooses business travellers as its focus." → **Targeting**
> "It then builds its brand around 'the fastest check-in in the industry'." → **Positioning**

**Trap:**
- **Segmentation vs Targeting**: identifying groups ≠ choosing a group.
- **Targeting vs Positioning**: choosing *who* ≠ deciding *how you're perceived*.
- **Positioning vs Promotion**: positioning is the strategic mental "spot" you occupy; promotion is just one tool (advertising, PR) used to get there. Positioning is not merely physical placement or an ad campaign.

---

### 1.3 Ansoff Matrix

**Zero:** A 2×2 grid asking: is the *product* new or existing, and is the *market* new or existing?

**Hero:**

| | Existing Product | New Product |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing Market** | Market Penetration (lowest risk) | Product Development |
| **New Market** | Market Development | Diversification (highest risk) |

- **Market Penetration** — sell more of what you have, to who you already sell to (e.g. loyalty programs, promotions).
- **Product Development** — new/modified product, same customer base (e.g. a premium tier for existing subscribers).
- **Market Development** — same product, new market/geography/segment (e.g. launching in a new country).
- **Diversification** — new product, new market — riskiest because you have no existing expertise on *either* axis.

**Why Market Development is usually riskier than Product Development** (a very common exam distinction):
1. You know nothing first-hand about the new customer base (preferences, price sensitivity, culture).
2. New regulatory/operational/logistics environment (vs staying inside a supply chain you already control).
3. Brand equity may not transfer — you're building awareness from zero.
4. More variables sit *outside* your control (local competitors, distribution partners) vs product development where the firm controls most of the risk factors (its own design, pricing, positioning).

**Trap:** Diversification is not "any risky move" — it specifically requires *both* axes to be new. If either the product or the market is something the firm already knows, it's not diversification, however risky it feels.

---

### 1.4 Value Proposition Canvas

**Zero:** Two matching halves — what the *customer* is dealing with, and what the *firm* offers to address it.

**Hero:**

Customer side:
- **Jobs-to-be-done** — the task/goal the customer is trying to accomplish.
- **Pains** — problems, frustrations, risks they experience.
- **Gains** — outcomes/benefits they want.

Firm side (must map 1:1 onto the above):
- **Products/Services** — what's actually offered.
- **Pain Relievers** — how the offering removes/reduces a specific pain.
- **Gain Creators** — how the offering produces a specific gain.

**Fit** exists only when relievers/creators genuinely match a real job/pain/gain — not just when the firm lists features it's proud of.

**Trap:**
- **Job vs Pain**: a Job is the underlying goal ("get to work on time"); a Pain is what gets in the way of it ("unreliable public transport").
- **Feature vs Gain**: "24/7 customer support" is a *feature* (firm side); "feeling reassured that help is always available" is the *gain* (customer side). Don't answer "what's the gain?" with a product feature.

---

### 1.5 Types of Value

**Zero:** Customers weigh up four kinds of value against what they give up.

**Hero:**
- **Functional value** — what the product literally does (e.g. lean meat = nutrition).
- **Symbolic value** — what owning/using it says about the customer (e.g. ethically-sourced goods signal identity/status).
- **Experiential value** — how it feels to consume it (enjoyment, atmosphere, excitement).
- **Cost/Sacrifice value** — the *total* burden: price **+** time **+** effort **+** inconvenience **+** risk.

**Trap:** Cost/sacrifice ≠ price. A cheap product with a painful, slow purchase process still has high "cost" to the customer. Any offering can carry more than one value type simultaneously — don't force a single label.

---

### 1.6 Create Shared Value (CSV)

**Zero:** Making money and solving a social problem *through the same activity* — not two separate activities.

**Hero:** CSV is embedded in the core business model/value-creation process itself (e.g. redesigning a supply chain so it's both cheaper *and* reduces farmer poverty).

**Trap:** CSV ≠ CSR/philanthropy. Donating profits to charity is *not* CSV — there's no link between the social good and how the firm actually creates economic value. If the social benefit could be removed without touching the business model, it's not CSV.

---

### 1.7 Marketing Mix (4Ps)

**Zero:** Product, Price, Place, Promotion — the full toolkit of decisions, not just advertising.

**Trap:** "Marketing" is not a synonym for "promotion." A pricing decision or a distribution decision is still a *marketing* decision.

## PART 2 — WEEK 5: DELIVERING & RETAINING VALUE

### 2.1 Porter's Value Chain

**Zero:** Every activity a firm performs either sits in the direct chain that makes/delivers the product (Primary) or supports that chain (Support).

**Hero:**

Primary: Inbound Logistics → Operations → Outbound Logistics → Marketing & Sales → Service

Support: Procurement, Technology Development, HR Management, Firm Infrastructure

**Trap:** Support activities are **not** unimportant — HR, tech and procurement can be genuine sources of competitive advantage and cost savings. Don't dismiss them as "just admin."

---

### 2.2 Customer Journey

**Zero:** Customers pass through stages on the way to becoming loyal.

**Hero:** Awareness → Interest → Desire → **Action** → **Retention**

**Trap — Action vs Retention:** buying **once** = Action. Buying **repeatedly**/staying loyal = Retention. Don't conflate "made a purchase" with "is retained."

---

### 2.3 Movement Effects vs Level Effects

**Zero:** Two different ways to grow.

**Hero:**
- **Movement effect** — improving a touchpoint that nudges customers to the *next stage* (e.g. better ads move people from Awareness → Interest).
- **Level effect** — increasing the *proportion retained* at the loyalty stage. Because acquiring a new customer is typically far more expensive than keeping an existing one, small improvements in retention rate can be disproportionately valuable.

**Trap:** Don't assume all growth interventions are equal — a retention-focused campaign works on the *level* at the end of the funnel, not on moving people between early stages.

---

### 2.4 Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

**Zero:** Total economic worth of a customer across the *whole relationship*, not just one transaction.

**Trap:** A high one-off sale doesn't necessarily mean a high CLV — a smaller but recurring purchaser can be worth more over time. CLV is why retention matters economically.

---

### 2.5 Distribution Channels

**Zero:** Channels exist to bridge gaps of **time, place, and possession** between producer and customer.

**Hero:**

| | Direct (Firm → Customer) | Indirect (Firm → Intermediary → Customer) |
|---|---|---|
| Pros | More control, more customer data, tighter experience control | Greater reach, scalability, access to existing networks |
| Cons | Limited reach/scale | Less control, less direct data, margin shared with intermediary |

**Trap:** Neither is "always better" — direct is not always more expensive per unit, and indirect is not automatically cheaper. It depends on case facts (scale needed, control needed, existing network access). Also don't confuse distribution decisions with promotion decisions.

---

### 2.6 Churn

**Zero:** The rate at which customers stop buying / leave.

**Trap:** Churn and retention are two sides of the same coin — always connect a churn number back to what it implies for CLV and acquisition cost, not just report the number in isolation.

---

## PART 3 — WEEK 7: PRICING & CVP (COST-VOLUME-PROFIT) ACCOUNTING

### 3.1 Pricing Tripod

**Zero:** Three forces bound every pricing decision.

**Hero:**
- **Costs** → the **floor** (you can't sustainably price below this).
- **Competitors** → the competitive context (what alternatives cost).
- **Customer value** → the **ceiling** (what customers will actually pay, regardless of your costs).

**Trap:** High costs don't justify a high price if customers don't perceive that much value — customer-perceived value caps the price, not the firm's cost structure. Similarly, matching competitors blindly ignores your own cost floor and unique value.

---

### 3.2 Fixed vs Variable Costs

**Zero:**
- **Variable costs** change with volume (materials, per-unit production cost).
- **Fixed costs** stay constant *within the relevant range* (rent, some salaries).

**Trap (the single most tested Week 7 trap):** Fixed costs are a **lump sum** in CVP analysis — do NOT treat "fixed cost per unit" as a real per-unit cost that stays the same. Fixed cost *per unit* changes as volume changes (spread over more/fewer units) even though *total* fixed cost doesn't. Also remember "constant" only holds within the relevant range — outside it, fixed costs can step up (e.g. needing a second factory).

---

### 3.3 Contribution Margin (CM)

**Formula:**
```
CM per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per Unit
```

**Zero:** CM is what's left from each sale after variable costs, available first to cover fixed costs, then to generate profit.

**Worked example:** Selling price $50, variable cost $30 → CM = $20/unit.

**Trap:** CM ≠ profit. CM only becomes profit *after* total fixed costs are covered.

---

### 3.4 Break-Even Point (BEP)

**Formula:**
```
BEP (units) = Total Fixed Costs / CM per unit
```

**Zero:** At break-even, Profit = 0, and Total Contribution = Total Fixed Costs.

**Worked example:** Fixed costs $100,000, CM/unit $20 → BEP = 5,000 units.

**Trap:** If fixed costs rise → BEP rises. If price rises (CM rises) → BEP falls. If variable cost rises (CM falls) → BEP rises. Always reason through CM first, not straight from price/cost to BEP.

---

### 3.5 Weighted Average CM / Multi-Product CVP

**Formula:**
```
Weighted Average CM = Σ (Product CM × Sales Mix %)
```

**Zero:** When a firm sells multiple products, BEP depends not just on total volume but on the *mix* of what's sold.

**Trap — heavily tested edge case:** If the sales mix shifts toward more low-margin products (even if total unit volume is unchanged or higher), Weighted Average CM **falls**, BEP **rises**, and profitability generally **worsens**. Total volume alone does not tell you profitability — you must check the mix.

---

### 3.6 Target Profit

**Formula:**
```
Units = (Fixed Costs + Target Pre-Tax Profit) / CM per unit
```

**Zero:** Contribution must cover fixed costs *and* the desired profit — so you add target profit into the numerator alongside fixed costs.

---

### 3.7 After-Tax Target Profit (the #1 exam trap in Week 7)

**Formula:**
```
Pre-Tax Profit = After-Tax Profit / (1 − Tax Rate)

Units = (Fixed Costs + Pre-Tax Target Profit) / CM per unit
```

**Zero:** If a target is stated *after tax*, you must gross it back up to a pre-tax figure **before** plugging it into the CVP formula.

**Worked example:** Desired after-tax profit = $70,000, tax rate = 30%.
Pre-tax profit = 70,000 / (1 − 0.30) = 70,000 / 0.70 = **$100,000**
Then: Units = (Fixed Costs + 100,000) / CM per unit.

**Trap:** Never plug the after-tax figure directly into the target-profit formula — this is the exact mistake examiners design MCQs to catch. Always ask first: "is this profit figure before or after tax?"

---

### 3.8 Value Analysis (VA/NVA)

**Zero:**
- **Value-adding (VA)** activities contribute to customer-perceived value and are necessary to deliver the offering.
- **Non-value-adding (NVA)** activities consume resources without meaningfully increasing customer-perceived value — candidates for reduction/elimination.

**Trap:** "Necessary for the firm to operate" ≠ "value-adding to the customer." An activity can be operationally required (e.g. internal compliance paperwork) while still being non-value-adding from the *customer's* perspective. Judge VA/NVA from the customer's point of view, not the firm's convenience.

## PART 4 — HOW TO ANSWER CASE-STUDY QUESTIONS (Part 2, 40 marks)

Use this chain every time, for every marketing or accounting response:

```
Concept → Case Evidence → Analysis → Implication → Recommendation
```

**Weak answer (loses marks):**
> "The company should target young people because they are a good market."

**Strong answer (full marks):**
> "The company should target price-sensitive university students because the case indicates affordability is their primary pain and existing competitors are positioned toward premium customers. This creates an opportunity to position the firm's low-cost offering around affordability and convenience."

Difference: the strong answer names the *framework* (targeting/positioning), cites *specific case facts*, explains the *reasoning* (why the evidence supports the conclusion), and ends in a clear, justified *recommendation*. A definition alone, without applying it to the case, earns little to no marks in Part 2.

## PART 5 — FINAL-DAY TRAP CHECKLIST

Run through this list the morning of the exam — these are the exact distinctions the syllabus flags as deliberately tested:

- [ ] Segmentation vs Targeting vs Positioning vs Promotion
- [ ] Product Development vs Market Development (and why Market Development is generally riskier)
- [ ] Diversification requires *both* axes new — not just "risky"
- [ ] Job vs Pain vs Gain; Feature vs Gain Creator
- [ ] Functional vs Symbolic vs Experiential value; Cost/Sacrifice ≠ price alone
- [ ] CSV vs CSR/charity
- [ ] Primary vs Support value-chain activities (support ≠ unimportant)
- [ ] Action vs Retention in the customer journey
- [ ] Movement effect vs Level effect
- [ ] Direct vs Indirect distribution — neither is "always better"
- [ ] Fixed cost is a lump sum, NOT a stable per-unit cost
- [ ] CM ≠ profit
- [ ] Effect of price/variable cost/fixed cost changes on CM and BEP
- [ ] Sales mix shifting toward low-margin products → weighted CM falls, BEP rises
- [ ] After-tax target profit must be grossed up to pre-tax BEFORE the CVP formula
- [ ] VA/NVA judged from the customer's perspective, not firm convenience
