# WEEK 5 — International Trade & Globalisation

## 1. The Big Picture

The core puzzle this week answers: **if countries are different, should they trade, and who wins?**

The intuitive (wrong) answer most students bring into this topic is "a country should only export things it's good at, and only trade with countries that are worse than it overall." The entire point of Week 5 is to demolish that intuition and replace it with the idea that **trade is driven by relative, not absolute, differences** — and that gains from trade exist even between a very productive country and a very unproductive one.

This connects to the course's broader learning outcome of evaluating global business environments because it explains *why* supply chains are structured the way they are (why iPhones are designed in California, assembled in China, with chips from Taiwan), and why firms and governments make locational decisions like offshoring or friendshoring.

## 2. The Logic — Why Trade Happens

### Absolute Advantage vs. Comparative Advantage (the core mechanism)

**Absolute advantage** is simple: who produces more output per hour of labour? If Australia can produce more of *everything* than Fiji, Australia has an absolute advantage in everything.

But absolute advantage tells you almost nothing about what should actually be traded. The variable that determines the **pattern of trade** is **opportunity cost** — what you give up to produce one more unit of something.

**Why this matters mechanically:** Imagine Australia is better than Fiji at producing *both* wine and cloth. It still pays for Australia to specialise in whichever good it is *relatively* better at (lower opportunity cost) and import the other, because:
- Every hour Australia spends making cloth is an hour *not* spent making wine — and Australia's wine-making skill is so much higher than Fiji's that the "opportunity cost" of diverting labour into cloth is very high for Australia.
- Fiji, even though it's worse at both, has a *comparatively* lower opportunity cost of making cloth (it isn't giving up much wine production, because it wasn't very good at wine anyway).

Result: **both countries can end up consuming more of both goods after specialising and trading** than either could produce alone. This is the mathematically provable core result of trade theory — the "gains from trade" are real and mutual, not zero-sum.

**The wage connection:** Absolute advantage doesn't determine what a country exports, but it *does* determine average wages. Highly productive economies (US, Australia, Germany) have high wages because their absolute productivity is high — regardless of what specific goods they specialise in exporting.

### Economies of Scale — Why Firms Cluster

This is a *second, separate* reason for trade that has nothing to do with comparative advantage — it explains why similar countries trade similar goods with each other (e.g. Germany exports cars to Japan and imports cars from Japan).

- **Internal economies of scale:** As a single *firm* grows bigger, its average cost per unit falls (spreading fixed costs, bulk input discounts, specialised machinery). This creates **intra-industry trade** — countries trading variants of the same product (BMW vs. Toyota) rather than fundamentally different goods. Consumers gain from more product variety, not just lower prices.
- **External economies of scale:** As an entire *industry* clusters geographically, every firm in that cluster benefits — regardless of its own individual size — because of a shared pool of specialised labour, an ecosystem of specialised suppliers, and knowledge spillovers between firms sitting near each other (think Silicon Valley for tech, or the City of London for finance).

**The strategic tension this creates — the Proximity-Concentration Trade-off:** Firms face two pulling forces:
- *Concentration* pulls them toward one location to capture economies of scale.
- *Proximity* pulls them toward being near their customers, to cut transport costs and respond faster to local demand.

Firms resolve this trade-off differently depending on transport costs, trade barriers, and how "weight-losing" or perishable their product is.

## 3. Key Terminology

| Term | Plain-English Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Offshoring** | A firm relocates part of its own production process abroad (still the same company) — usually to cut costs |
| **Outsourcing** | Contracting a *separate* firm (domestic or foreign) to do part of the production |
| **Friendshoring** | Deliberately shifting supply chains toward politically "trusted" partner countries, prioritising geopolitical security over pure cost minimisation |
| **Tariff** | A tax on imports; raises the price of the foreign good to protect domestic producers, but raises costs for domestic consumers/firms that rely on the import |
| **Intra-industry trade** | Trading similar goods within the same industry (cars for cars) — explained by economies of scale, not comparative advantage |
| **Inter-industry trade** | Trading fundamentally different goods (wine for cloth) — explained by comparative advantage |

## 4. Typical Exam Traps

- **"The Productivity Myth":** A question will describe a country that is worse at producing *everything* and ask if trade still benefits it. The correct answer is always **yes** — comparative advantage guarantees gains from trade even for the absolutely less productive country, as long as opportunity costs differ.
- **Confusing what determines wages vs. what determines trade pattern:** Absolute advantage → wages. Comparative advantage → what gets exported. Exam questions love to swap these.
- **Ignoring the losers:** The *country* gains in aggregate from trade, but specific industries/workers with a comparative *disadvantage* face real income losses and dislocation. If a question asks "does everyone benefit from trade?" — the answer distinguishing *aggregate* national benefit from *individual* worker harm is the nuance they're testing.
- **Assuming internal and external economies of scale are the same thing.** Internal = firm-size-driven (leads to a few dominant firms + intra-industry trade). External = industry-cluster-driven (can help even small firms, and explains *geographic clustering* like Silicon Valley, not just trade patterns).

# WEEK 7 — Long-Run Trends: Growth & Inequality

## 1. The Big Picture

This week explains the "hockey stick" of human history: living standards were roughly flat for thousands of years, then, starting around the Industrial Revolution, exploded upward in some countries and not others. The central question is: **what makes a country rich, and what makes it grow?**

We use **development accounting** to split GDP into two possible sources: how much *stuff* (capital and labour) a country has, versus how *smart* it is at using that stuff (technology/ideas). This distinction matters enormously for policy — pouring money into infrastructure works differently than investing in R&D and education.

## 2. The Cobb-Douglas Production Function — Fully Broken Down

$$Y = A \times K^{\alpha} \times (h \times L)^{1-\alpha}$$

Let's take this apart term by term.

- **$Y$ — Total Output (GDP).** The total value of everything the economy produces in a period.
- **$A$ — Total Factor Productivity (TFP).** This is the "ideas" or "technology" term — it captures everything that lets an economy produce *more output from the same inputs*: better institutions, better technology, better management practices, better infrastructure quality. It's the multiplier on everything else.
- **$K$ — Physical Capital.** Machines, factories, buildings, infrastructure — the physical tools of production.
- **$h$ — Human Capital per worker.** Skills, education, training — how much a given worker can produce due to their accumulated knowledge.
- **$L$ — Labour (raw headcount).** The number of workers.
- **$\alpha$ (alpha) — Capital's share of output**, a number between 0 and 1 (typically estimated around 1/3 in real economies). This exponent governs *how much extra output you get from adding more capital*, and correspondingly $(1-\alpha)$ is labour's share of output.

**What "return to scale" means here:** Because the exponents $\alpha$ and $(1-\alpha)$ sum to exactly 1, this function has **constant returns to scale** — if you double *both* capital and (human-capital-adjusted) labour simultaneously, output exactly doubles. This is a deliberate modelling assumption: it says the economy isn't inherently getting more or less efficient just from being bigger, holding technology fixed.

**Why the exponent on $K$ matters (diminishing returns):** Because $\alpha < 1$, each *additional* unit of capital added (holding labour and technology fixed) increases output by *less* than the previous unit did. This is **diminishing marginal returns to capital** — a single worker with one computer is enormously more productive than with zero computers, but giving that same worker a second, third, or tenth computer adds progressively less and less to what they can produce. Eventually more machines without more workers to run them, or new ideas to make them more useful, adds almost nothing.

**Output per worker:** Dividing through by $L$ gives you $Y/L$ (GDP per capita/worker) as a function of $A$, capital *per worker* ($K/L$), and human capital per worker ($h$). This is the version used to explain why rich and poor countries differ in living standards — it's rarely about raw headcount of workers, it's about capital-per-worker, skill-per-worker, and technology.

## 3. The Solow-Swan Model — Full Detail

Solow-Swan asks: if a country just keeps saving and investing more, does it stay rich forever, get richer forever, or hit a wall?

### The mechanics

- **Capital accumulation:** Each period, a country's capital stock grows via **investment** (a fraction $s$ — the savings rate — of output $Y$ is saved and turned into new capital) and shrinks via **depreciation** (a fraction $\delta$ of the existing capital stock wears out every period, and — in the full model — capital per worker also falls if the population/labour force $L$ grows, because the same capital stock now has to be spread across more workers).
- The change in capital per worker each period is essentially: *new investment per worker* **minus** *(depreciation + population growth) × existing capital per worker*.

### Why capital hits diminishing returns (and why this is the crux of the whole model)

Because of the Cobb-Douglas $K^\alpha$ term above, each additional unit of capital per worker raises output per worker by less than the last one. But the amount that needs to be reinvested just to *keep up* with depreciation and population growth scales up roughly linearly with the capital stock. 

The consequence: as capital per worker rises, the "investment needed just to stand still" line and the "new investment generated by savings" curve eventually intersect. At that point, capital per worker **stops growing** — this is the **steady state**.

### The Steady State

At the steady state:
- Capital per worker ($K/L$) is constant.
- Output per worker ($Y/L$) is therefore also constant.
- The economy is *not* shrinking — it's just no longer growing in *per-person* terms. Total output can still grow if the population grows (more workers, same output per worker), but living standards per person plateau.

### Impact of a higher savings rate

Raising the savings rate $s$ shifts the whole economy to a **higher** steady-state level of capital and output per worker — it's a genuine, permanent boost to the standard of living. But critically, it is a **one-off level shift**, not a source of permanently higher *growth*. Once the new (higher) steady state is reached, per-capita growth returns to zero without further changes in technology.

### Impact of population growth

Faster population growth means the existing capital stock has to be spread across more people, so a higher fraction of savings goes just to equipping new workers rather than deepening capital per existing worker. This pushes the steady state to a **lower** level of capital and output per worker, all else equal — it's a big part of why some models predict poorer countries with high birth rates converge to lower income levels.

### The punchline / limitation of Solow-Swan

**Capital accumulation alone cannot generate sustained long-run growth**, because diminishing returns always eventually catch up and drag the economy to a flat steady state. Solow-Swan's own conclusion is that the *only* way to keep growing forever is continuous improvement in $A$ (technology) — but the base model treats $A$ as falling from the sky (exogenous), unexplained. This gap is exactly what the Romer model fills in.

## 4. The Romer Model — Endogenous Growth

Romer's key move is to stop treating technology ($A$) as an unexplained external gift and instead model *where ideas come from* and *why they don't run into the same diminishing-returns wall that capital does*.

### Non-rivalry — the central insight

Physical capital is **rival**: if a factory is making shoes, that factory can't simultaneously be making cars. Capital used one way is capital that *cannot* be used another way at the same time.

**Ideas are non-rival.** Once someone invents a better manufacturing process, or writes a piece of software, or discovers a scientific principle, that idea can be used by an unlimited number of firms and people *simultaneously* without being "used up." Your neighbour using the Pythagorean theorem does not leave less of it for you.

This single property is why ideas don't hit the same diminishing-returns ceiling that capital does. Every new idea can combine with the entire existing stock of knowledge — a phenomenon often summarised as "standing on the shoulders of giants." The bigger the existing stock of ideas, the more raw material there is for the *next* idea, potentially making idea-generation get easier (or at least not harder) as the stock of knowledge grows.

### Knowledge spillovers

Because ideas are non-rival and hard to fully contain, innovations by one firm often "spill over" and boost the productivity of *other* firms too — even competitors — through imitation, reverse engineering, employee mobility, or published research. This is a positive externality: the *private* return to inventing something new is usually smaller than the *social* return, which is a key reason governments subsidise R&D, universities, and patents (patents are a deliberate, temporary exception — a government-granted *rivalry* over an idea, to preserve the incentive to invent it in the first place).

### Why Romer explains sustained growth where Solow-Swan can't

Because the stock of ideas ($A$) isn't subject to the same diminishing-returns dynamic as capital, continuous investment in research and idea-generation can push $A$ upward **indefinitely**, and since $A$ multiplies the *entire* production function, growth in $A$ translates into permanent, unbounded growth in output per worker — not just a one-off level shift like a higher savings rate does.

## 5. Distinctions Table — The Comparisons Examiners Test

| Concept A | Concept B | The Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Solow-Swan** | **Romer** | Solow-Swan: growth from capital accumulation, hits diminishing returns, converges to a flat steady state. Romer: growth from non-rival ideas, no diminishing-returns ceiling, generates *sustained* growth. |
| **Capital Deepening** | **Technological Progress** | Capital deepening = more machines per worker → diminishing returns, one-off level increase. Technological progress = better ideas → no diminishing returns, permanent growth-rate increase. |
| **Level Effect** | **Growth Rate Effect** | A level effect (e.g. raising the savings rate) permanently raises *how much* you produce, but growth returns to its old rate once the new steady state is hit. A growth-rate effect (e.g. faster idea generation) permanently changes the *slope* — the economy keeps growing faster forever. |
| **Absolute Advantage (Week 5 callback)** | **Comparative Advantage** | Just a reminder this distinction recurs across weeks — don't let the trade concepts and growth concepts blur together on exam day. |
| **Rival Good** | **Non-Rival Good** | Rival = using it prevents someone else from using it (capital, land, labour). Non-rival = one person's use doesn't diminish availability for others (ideas, software, blueprints). |
| **Nominal GDP** | **Real GDP** | Nominal = measured in that year's prices (inflation included). Real = adjusted to constant prices, so it isolates genuine changes in quantity of output. |
| **Stock** | **Flow** | GDP per capita is a *flow* (income earned over a period). Wealth is a *stock* (accumulated assets at a point in time). Don't call GDP per capita a measure of wealth. |

## 6. Typical Exam Traps

- **"More savings = permanently faster growth."** False. In Solow-Swan, a higher savings rate raises the *level* of output per worker but does **not** raise the long-run *growth rate* — growth reverts to zero (in per-capita terms) once the new steady state is reached.
- **Treating capital and ideas as subject to the same constraints.** A question may ask "why can't a country get rich just by building more factories forever?" — the answer is diminishing marginal returns to capital, *specifically because capital is rival and $\alpha < 1$*. Ideas don't face this constraint because they're non-rival.
- **The Average Trap:** A country can post a high GDP per capita while most citizens are poor, if income inequality (commonly measured by the **Gini Index**) is high. GDP per capita is a *mean*, not a distribution.
- **Confusing catch-up growth with frontier growth.** Poor countries can grow very fast temporarily by *importing* existing foreign technology and rapidly accumulating capital (catch-up growth) — this is not the same mechanism as a rich, frontier country generating brand-new ideas via Romer-style endogenous growth. Catch-up growth also eventually slows as the "easy" imported technology gap closes.
- **Skill-Biased Technological Change**: technology doesn't raise all wages equally — it disproportionately raises the productivity (and wages) of high-skilled workers, which is a commonly tested reason for *rising* inequality alongside growth, not something that only affects poor countries.

# WEEK 8 — Short-Run Fluctuations & Policy

## 1. The Big Picture

If Week 7 is the smooth long-run trendline, Week 8 zooms in on the wiggles around it — the **business cycle**. The core question: **why does spending falling cause unemployment (rather than just lower prices), and what can policy do about it?**

## 2. The Logic — Why Demand Shocks Cause Recessions

### Sticky prices and wages — the mechanism that makes this model different from a simple supply-and-demand story

In a perfectly flexible market, a drop in demand would just cause prices to fall until the market clears — no lasting unemployment. The Keynesian insight underlying this week is that **prices and especially wages are "sticky"** in the short run: firms don't cut prices instantly, and employers are very reluctant to cut wages (due to contracts, morale effects, and menu costs).

Because prices can't adjust quickly to absorb a fall in spending, **firms cut production and lay off workers instead** — the adjustment happens through *quantities* (output, jobs) rather than *prices*, at least in the short run. This is the core justification for the Income-Expenditure model.

### The Income-Expenditure Model

$$Y = C + I + G + (EX - IM)$$

- $Y$ — total output/income
- $C$ — Consumption spending by households
- $I$ — Investment spending by firms
- $G$ — Government spending
- $(EX - IM)$ — Net exports (exports minus imports)

If any component drops (say, households get scared and cut consumption $C$), and prices don't adjust to compensate, then $Y$ itself falls — meaning less output is produced and workers are laid off.

### The Multiplier — why a small shock becomes a big one

$$\Delta Y = \text{Initial Change} \times \frac{1}{1-a}$$

where $a$ is the **Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)** — the fraction of every extra dollar of income a household spends (as opposed to saves).

**The mechanism:** if the government spends an extra $100, that $100 becomes income for someone (a builder, say), who then spends a fraction $a$ of it (say $80 if MPC = 0.8), which becomes income for someone else, who spends $64 of *that*, and so on. The initial $100 injection ripples through the economy multiple times over, producing a *total* increase in output larger than the initial spending itself. The higher the MPC, the bigger the multiplier — because more of each round of income gets re-spent instead of saved.

### The Fisher Equation — nominal vs. real interest rates

$$i = r + \pi^e$$

- $i$ — the **nominal** interest rate (the rate you actually see quoted at the bank)
- $r$ — the **real** interest rate (adjusted for expected inflation — this is what actually drives real decisions)
- $\pi^e$ — expected inflation

**Why this matters for policy:** what actually determines whether a business decides to invest, or a household decides to borrow for a house, is the *real* cost of borrowing ($r$), not the nominal rate. If expected inflation rises but the nominal rate stays the same, the real rate has effectively *fallen* — borrowing has gotten cheaper in real terms, even though the sticker rate hasn't changed. This is why central banks pay close attention to inflation *expectations*, not just current inflation.

### Monetary Transmission — how an RBA rate cut actually reaches GDP

A cash rate cut doesn't boost GDP directly — it flows through several channels:
1. **Borrowing costs** fall, encouraging firms to invest and households to spend (especially on big-ticket, credit-financed items like housing).
2. **Asset prices** (shares, housing) tend to rise when rates fall, boosting household wealth and — via the **wealth effect** — encouraging more consumption spending even without any change in income.
3. **Exchange rate depreciation** — lower domestic interest rates make the currency relatively less attractive to foreign investors, causing it to depreciate, which makes exports cheaper and imports more expensive, boosting net exports $(EX - IM)$.

## 3. Key Terminology

| Term | Plain-English Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Consumption Smoothing** | Households try to keep a roughly stable standard of living over their lifetime by borrowing when young/income is low and saving when income is high, rather than letting consumption swing wildly with current income |
| **Automatic Stabilisers** | Built-in fiscal features (progressive income tax, unemployment benefits) that automatically dampen the business cycle without any new legislation — tax revenue falls and benefit payouts rise automatically during a downturn, cushioning the fall in disposable income |
| **Headline Inflation** | The full, unadjusted CPI figure — includes volatile items like fuel and fresh food |
| **Underlying Inflation** | A "trimmed" measure that strips out temporary/volatile price swings to reveal the persistent inflation trend — this is what the RBA actually targets, not the noisy headline number |
| **Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)** | The practical floor near 0% on nominal interest rates, below which conventional monetary policy loses traction, forcing central banks toward "unconventional" tools |
| **Quantitative Easing (QE)** | A central bank buying large quantities of financial assets (usually government bonds) to inject liquidity and lower longer-term interest rates when the cash rate is already near zero |

## 4. Exam Traps

- **Fiscal Policy Lags:** A common misconception is that government spending can respond to a recession instantly. In reality there are three distinct lags: **recognition lag** (realising a recession is happening), **decision lag** (passing the legislation/budget), and **implementation lag** (money actually being spent on the ground) — collectively meaning stimulus often arrives *after* the economy has already started recovering on its own, or even worsens the *next* cycle by overheating a recovery.
- **The Zero Lower Bound trap:** if a question describes an economy where the cash rate is already at (or near) 0% and demand is still falling, this signals that standard interest-rate cuts are exhausted, and the correct policy response category is **unconventional monetary policy** (QE) or fiscal policy — not "cut rates further."
- **Unexpected inflation and debt:** if inflation turns out *higher* than expected, the *real* value of a fixed nominal debt shrinks — meaning **borrowers benefit and lenders lose** (they're repaid in dollars that buy less than they anticipated). This is commonly flipped in trick questions.
- **Sticky prices vs. flexible prices:** don't confuse this model with a standard microeconomic supply-and-demand adjustment. The entire Week 8 framework only makes sense *because* prices/wages are assumed sticky in the short run — that assumption is what turns a demand shock into unemployment rather than just a one-off price adjustment.
- **Multiplier size confusion:** a *higher* MPC produces a *bigger* multiplier (more re-spending each round), not a smaller one. Also remember the multiplier formula uses $(1-a)$ in the denominator, where $a$ is MPC — a common error is inverting this or forgetting the "1 minus."
