## SECTION A — CONCEPTUAL MCQs (20 marks)

**A1.** An investment doubles in value over 9 years with annual compounding. A student divides the total percentage gain (100%) by 9 years to get "11.1% p.a." Which statement best describes the error?

a) The calculation is correct because compounding and simple growth produce the same implied rate
b) The calculation understates the true compound annual rate, because it ignores that early returns themselves earn further returns — the correct approach is to solve r = (FV/PV)^(1/n) − 1
c) The calculation overstates the true compound annual rate
d) There is no way to solve for an implied annual rate without a financial calculator

**Answer: (c)**

**Working:**
True compound rate solves (1+r)^9 = 2 → r = 2^(1/9) − 1 = 0.0801 = **8.0% p.a.**

The naive method gives 100% ÷ 9 = **11.1% p.a.**

Since 11.1% > 8.0%, the naive linear method **overstates** the true rate — a smaller compound rate is needed to double your money than a simple straight-line average suggests, because compounding lets growth build on growth (i.e. you don't need as much "extra" percentage each year once earlier gains are already working for you).

⚠️ **Trap:** Option (b) has the *correct formula* r = (FV/PV)^(1/n) − 1, but the wrong conclusion ("understates"). This is a classic exam trap — one part of an option is right, one part is wrong, and the whole option is therefore incorrect. Always check every clause, not just the formula.

**A2.** Perpetuity ($5,000/yr forever) vs. ordinary annuity ($5,000/yr for 40 years), same discount rate.

**Answer: (b)**

PV(annuity) = C/r × [1 − (1+r)⁻ⁿ]. As n → ∞, the bracket term → 1, so PV(annuity) → C/r = PV(perpetuity). A perpetuity is mathematically the *limit* of an annuity as its life extends to infinity — so its PV is always ≥ any finite annuity's PV, with the gap shrinking as n grows.

---

**A3.** Comparing Machine X (3-yr life) and Machine Y (5-yr life), both positive but different NPVs.

**Answer: (b)** — Equivalent Annual Amount (EAA). Raw NPVs aren't comparable across unequal lives because they don't reflect value "per year" the asset is in service.

---

**A4.** Project A: NPV $60,000. Project B: NPV $95,000, larger investment, lower IRR.

**Answer: (b)** — For mutually exclusive projects, **NPV is the correct decision criterion**, not IRR. IRR is a % return and ignores scale — a lower % return on a much larger investment can still create more total dollar value.

---

**A5.** Capital rationing and Profitability Index (PI).

**Answer: (b)** — PI = NPV/Initial investment (or PV of inflows/PV of outflows) measures value created *per dollar invested*, which is exactly what matters when the investment budget itself is the binding constraint.

---

**A6.** Shifting sales mix toward a lower-margin product because it "sells more units."

**Answer: (b)** — Weighted-average contribution margin falls, which can raise the break-even point and reduce total profit even with more units sold. Volume alone is not the right lens — margin composition matters.

---

**A7.** Enterprise Value (EV) error: adding cash instead of subtracting it.

**Answer: (b)** — EV = Market value of equity + Market value of debt − Cash. Cash is a non-operating asset; EV should reflect only the value of core operations. Adding cash instead of subtracting **overstates** EV (double error — it's not just "not subtracted," it's added, which is worse).

---

**A8.** Coupon rate = market YTM.

**Answer: (c)** — Bond trades at **par (face value)**. (Coupon > YTM → premium; coupon < YTM → discount.)

---

**A9.** Project with different risk profile from firm's typical operations (mining firm entering software).

**Answer: (b)** — Use a **project-specific discount rate** reflecting that project's own systematic risk (its own beta), not the firm's overall WACC. Using firm WACC on a higher-risk project would understate the required return and could lead to accepting value-destroying projects (and vice versa).

---

**A10.** Revenue growth achieved by cutting staff training and letting experienced employees leave, to hit a profit target.

**Answer: (b)** — **Human capital** is being eroded. Lesson: short-term financial capital (profit) can be boosted at the expense of another capital (workforce skill/capability), and this erosion often does *not* show up immediately in the financials — it shows up later as reduced capability, which is exactly why the Six Capitals framework matters.

## SECTION B — SHORT NUMERIC QUESTIONS (8 marks)

**B1.** Years for $6,000 to grow to $10,000 at 5.5% p.a. compounded annually.

FV = PV(1+r)ⁿ → n = ln(FV/PV) / ln(1+r)

n = ln(10,000/6,000) / ln(1.055) = ln(1.6667) / ln(1.055) = 0.5108 / 0.05354

**n ≈ 9.54 years**

(If the question wants whole compounding periods, it would take a full **10 years** for the balance to *reach or exceed* $10,000 — but the formula answer, showing full working, is 9.54 years.)

---

**B2.** APR = 21%, compounded monthly → EAR.

EAR = (1 + APR/m)ᵐ − 1 = (1 + 0.21/12)¹² − 1 = (1.0175)¹² − 1

**EAR ≈ 23.14%**

⚠️ Common mistake: reporting 21% as the "true" annual cost, or accidentally computing (1.21)^(1/12) instead of (1+0.21/12)^12.

---

**B3.** Sales mix: 3 units X (CM $20) : 2 units Y (CM $35). Weighted-average CM/unit.

Weighted-avg CM = (3 × 20 + 2 × 35) / (3 + 2) = (60 + 70) / 5 = 130/5

**= $26.00 per unit**

⚠️ Common mistake: taking a simple (unweighted) average of $20 and $35 = $27.50 — wrong, because it ignores the different sales proportions.

---

**B4.** Cost of debt (YTM) = 5.5%, tax rate = 30%. After-tax cost of debt.

rd(after-tax) = rd × (1 − t) = 5.5% × (1 − 0.30) = 5.5% × 0.70

**= 3.85%**

## SECTION C — SCENARIO-BASED QUANTITATIVE PROBLEMS (32 marks)

### Question C1 — Annuities and Perpetuities (7 marks)

**a. PV of Option 1** — $12,000 at the *beginning* of each year for 15 years, r = 6% (annuity due). (3 marks)

Ordinary annuity PV = C × [1 − (1+r)⁻ⁿ] / r
(1.06)¹⁵ ≈ 2.39656 → (1.06)⁻¹⁵ ≈ 0.41727

PV(ordinary) = 12,000 × [1 − 0.41727] / 0.06 = 12,000 × 9.7122 = $116,547

**Annuity due** adjustment (payments at start of year, one extra period of growth):
PV(due) = PV(ordinary) × (1+r) = 116,547 × 1.06

**≈ $123,540**

**b. PV of Option 2** — $12,000 forever, first payment in 1 year (ordinary perpetuity). (2 marks)

PV = C/r = 12,000/0.06

**= $200,000**

**c. Conceptual explanation** (2 marks)

The perpetuity's PV ($200,000) is substantially higher than the 15-year annuity due's PV (~$123,540) because a perpetuity is the limiting case of an annuity as the number of payments → ∞. An ordinary annuity's PV converges toward C/r as n grows — the 15-year annuity only captures a finite stream of discounted cash flows, while the perpetuity keeps adding (ever-smaller, but always positive) present values indefinitely. This more than offsets the annuity due's timing advantage (payments starting immediately rather than in year 1).

### Question C2 — Investment Decision Rules with Different Project Lives (6 marks)

**a. Why raw NPV comparison is flawed** (2 marks)

Machine A (4-yr life, NPV $85,000) and Machine B (7-yr life, NPV $115,000) generate value over different time horizons. Comparing raw NPVs unfairly favours the longer-life project simply because it has more years to accumulate value — it doesn't tell us which machine creates more value *per year* of service, which matters for mutually exclusive, repeatable choices.

**b. EAA for each machine** (3 marks)

EAA = NPV / Annuity factor

Machine A: EAA = 85,000 / 3.170 = **$26,814**
Machine B: EAA = 115,000 / 4.868 = **$23,624**

**c. Decision** (1 mark)

Choose **Machine A** — it has the higher Equivalent Annual Amount, meaning it creates more value per year, even though its raw NPV is lower.

### Question C3 — Equity Valuation and Return Decomposition (6 marks)

**a. Implied required return using Gordon Growth Model** (3 marks)

P0 = D1 / (re − g) → re = D1/P0 + g

re = 2.20/40 + 0.04 = 0.055 + 0.04

**re = 9.5%**

**b. Decomposition** (3 marks)

- **Dividend yield** = D1/P0 = 2.20/40 = **5.5%** — the cash return earned from dividends relative to the current share price.
- **Capital gains yield** = g = **4.0%** — the expected rate of price appreciation, which under Gordon Growth equals the constant dividend growth rate.
- Together: 5.5% + 4.0% = 9.5% = re ✓ (dividend yield + capital gains yield = total required return)

### Question C4 — WACC and CAPM Integration (7 marks)

**a. Cost of equity via CAPM** (2 marks)

re = rf + β(rm − rf) = 4% + 0.9 × (10% − 4%) = 4% + 0.9 × 6% = 4% + 5.4%

**re = 9.4%**

**b. Capital structure weights** (2 marks)

Total capital = $25m + $10m = $35m

- Weight of equity = 25/35 = **71.43%**
- Weight of debt = 10/35 = **28.57%**

**c. WACC** (2 marks)

WACC = We×re + Wd×rd×(1−t)
= 0.7143 × 9.4% + 0.2857 × 5% × (1 − 0.30)
= 6.714% + (0.2857 × 3.5%)
= 6.714% + 1.000%

**WACC ≈ 7.71%**

**d. Effect of a tax rate cut** (1 mark)

A lower tax rate reduces the value of the interest tax shield, so after-tax cost of debt [rd×(1−t)] **rises**. Holding rd and the capital structure weights constant, this pushes **WACC up**.

### Question C5 — CVP, Target Profit and Value Analysis (6 marks)

**a. Contribution margin and break-even** (2 marks)

CM/unit = Price − Variable cost = 220 − 130 = **$90/unit**

Break-even units = Fixed costs / CM = 315,000 / 90

**= 3,500 units**

**b. Units for target after-tax profit of $252,000** (3 marks)

Pre-tax profit required = After-tax profit / (1 − t) = 252,000 / (1 − 0.28) = 252,000 / 0.72

**= $350,000 pre-tax profit required**

Units needed = (Fixed costs + Required pre-tax profit) / CM = (315,000 + 350,000) / 90 = 665,000/90

**= 7,388.9 → 7,389 units** (round up — a fractional unit can't be sold, and rounding down would fall short of the target)

**c. Value analysis of the decorative carving** (1 mark)

This is a **non-value-added activity** — it consumes resources (labour/materials) but customers don't notice or value it, so it isn't reflected in willingness to pay. Recommendation: **eliminate or reduce** the hand-finishing carving to cut costs without reducing perceived customer value, and redirect that labour/time toward features customers *do* value (or straight to margin improvement).
